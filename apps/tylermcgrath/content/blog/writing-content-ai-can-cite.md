To write content AI engines quote, write passages that can stand alone. Start each section with a direct answer, define terms in plain "X is Y" sentences, name your subject instead of saying "it," and back every claim with a specific, verifiable detail. AI engines like ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews lift passages, not pages, so the paragraph is your unit of competition.

Most advice about AI visibility focuses on technical access, schema and brand mentions. Those matter. But when I look at which pages actually get quoted for a question, the deciding factor is often embarrassingly simple: one page had a clear, complete sentence that answered the question, and the others did not. This guide is about writing that sentence on purpose, at scale, across a whole site.

## Why AI Engines Quote Passages, Not Pages

AI answer engines quote passages because they assemble answers from the most relevant pieces of several sources rather than ranking whole documents. A page might be 3,000 words long, but only one or two paragraphs will match the specific question a user asked.

Retrieval-based systems generally work in stages. They find candidate pages, break them into smaller chunks, select the chunks that best match the question, and then generate an answer grounded in those chunks, often with a citation back to the source. The exact chunk sizes and scoring methods are proprietary and vary by provider, so I treat the details as unknown. The overall pattern, though, is well established, and it changes how you should write.

If the paragraph that contains your answer depends on the paragraph before it to make sense, it is weaker as a standalone chunk. If your best insight is buried in the middle of a long anecdote, it may never be selected. If your answer is correct but hedged into vagueness, the engine may prefer a source that says it plainly.

This is the core idea behind [generative engine optimization](/blog/what-is-generative-engine-optimization): you are not only competing to rank a page; you are competing to supply the best passage.

## The Anatomy of a Citable Passage

A citable passage is a short block of text that answers one question completely, in plain language, without needing outside context. In practice, the strongest passages share the same four-part shape.

1. **The direct answer.** One sentence that answers the question, ideally in the first 20 words.
2. **The reason or mechanism.** One or two sentences explaining why the answer is true.
3. **The specific detail.** A step, example, threshold, comparison or named entity that makes the answer concrete.
4. **The boundary.** When relevant, a short note on when the answer does not apply.

Here is a weak passage and a strong passage answering the same question, "How often should I update my Google Business Profile?"

> Weak: There are a lot of factors to consider here, and it really depends on your business. Generally speaking, it's a good idea to keep things fresh, and many experts agree that regular activity can be helpful in a variety of ways.

> Strong: Update your Google Business Profile whenever a customer-facing detail changes, and add a new post or photo at least every few weeks. Accurate hours, services and contact details prevent bad customer experiences, while fresh posts and photos show the profile is actively managed. Seasonal businesses should also update hours before every holiday period.

The strong version can be quoted in full, attributed and understood with zero context. The weak version cannot be quoted at all without making the engine look evasive.

## Write Definitions Engines Can Lift

A definitional sentence states what something is in a single, self-contained line, usually in the form "X is a Y that does Z." Definitions are among the most frequently lifted sentences in AI answers because "what is" questions are extremely common and engines need a clean, attributable statement to open with.

Good definitions follow a simple pattern:

- Name the term exactly as people search for it.
- Place it in a category ("is a framework," "is a file," "is a metric").
- State the distinguishing function or purpose.
- Keep it under about 30 words.

For example: "**Topical authority** is the depth and breadth of expertise a website demonstrates on a subject, built by covering that subject thoroughly through interlinked content." That sentence can be dropped into an answer as-is. (If you want the full treatment of that concept, see my guide to [topical authority and content clusters](/blog/topical-authority-guide).)

Place your definition early, ideally in the first paragraph or immediately after the first heading that introduces the term. Then expand. Do not open with history, a rhetorical question or a statement about how important the topic is.

## Make Every Section Self-Contained

A self-contained section is one that a reader, or an engine, could encounter alone and still fully understand. This is the single most important habit for AI-citable writing, and it is the one most writers resist because it feels repetitive.

Practical rules for self-contained sections:

- **Name the subject, not the pronoun.** Write "llms.txt is a proposed standard" instead of "It is a proposed standard," even if you mentioned llms.txt in the previous paragraph.
- **Restate the context in the heading.** "How to Measure AI Visibility" is better than "Measurement" because the heading itself carries meaning.
- **Avoid forward and backward references.** Phrases like "as mentioned above" or "we'll cover this below" break when a passage is extracted.
- **Open with the answer, not a transition.** "Another important point is that..." wastes the most valuable position in the section.
- **Keep paragraphs to two to four sentences.** Shorter paragraphs create cleaner natural boundaries for chunking.

Self-contained writing does produce some repetition across a long page. That is acceptable. A human reading top to bottom barely notices a repeated noun, but an engine extracting one section benefits enormously from it.

## Be Specific Enough to Be Worth Quoting

Specific claims are more citable than general ones because they give an engine something concrete to stand behind. "Page speed matters" is true and useless. "Google recommends a Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less for a good user experience" is a fact an engine can quote and attribute.

Specificity comes in several forms:

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "Post regularly." | "Publish a new post at least twice a month for the first six months." |
| "Use structured data." | "Add Organization schema with sameAs links to your official social profiles." |
| "Many AI crawlers exist." | "OpenAI documents three crawlers: GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot and ChatGPT-User." |
| "Results take time." | "Expect page-level changes to show within weeks and brand authority to build over months." |
| "Make it easy to read." | "Keep paragraphs to two to four sentences and use one idea per paragraph." |

Notice what specificity does not mean: it does not mean inventing numbers. If you do not have a verified statistic, use a concrete instruction, a named example or a clearly labeled illustration instead. A fabricated "73% of marketers" statistic might get repeated once, but it undermines trust in everything else you publish, and it is exactly the kind of thing that erodes [E-E-A-T](/blog/eeat-in-the-ai-era).

## Use Structure That Mirrors How People Ask

Structure helps engines find the right passage for the right question. The goal is not to decorate a page with formatting but to make each answer's location obvious.

### Headings that match real questions

Many prompts are phrased as questions: "how do I," "what is," "is it worth," "what's the difference between." Headings that echo those phrasings make it clear which section answers which question. Descriptive headings work too, as long as they accurately label what follows.

### Lists for steps and criteria

Numbered lists are ideal for processes because order matters and each step is a discrete, quotable unit. Bulleted lists work for criteria, options and checklists. Keep each list item to one idea and start it with a verb or a bolded label.

### Tables for comparisons

Comparison questions ("X vs Y," "best options for") are common in AI search, and tables present the answer in a compact, structured way. Use a clear header row and keep cell text short.

### Short summaries at the top

A key takeaways block or a two-to-three sentence summary near the top of the page gives engines a concise statement of your main points. It also helps readers who skim.

## Original Information Is the Strongest Citation Magnet

Original information is content that only your source can provide: your own data, test results, process, expert opinion or first-hand observation. AI engines can summarize the consensus from many sources, but when a question needs a fact that only one source has, that source tends to get the citation.

Forms of original information that work well:

- **Proprietary data.** Aggregated, anonymized findings from your own customers, projects or operations, published with a clear methodology.
- **Tests and comparisons.** You tried several approaches or products under defined conditions and report what happened.
- **Documented processes.** The exact steps your team follows, including the edge cases and mistakes, not a generic outline.
- **Expert positions.** A clear, defensible opinion from a named expert on a debated question, with the reasoning shown.
- **Definitions of your own frameworks.** If you coin a useful framework, define it clearly and consistently, and others may reference it.

When you publish data, always include who, what, when and how: the sample, the time period and the method. Undated, unsourced numbers are hard for anyone to cite responsibly.

## Common Writing Mistakes That Block Citations

The most common citation blockers are habits that feel like good writing in a traditional blog but work against extraction.

- **Long warm-up introductions.** Three paragraphs about "the evolving landscape" before the answer pushes your best content out of the strongest position.
- **Burying the answer in a story.** Anecdotes are valuable, but put the answer first and the story second.
- **Excessive hedging.** "It may possibly depend" signals low confidence. Be precise about uncertainty once, then state what you know.
- **Pronoun chains.** Paragraphs that start with "This," "It" or "That" lose meaning when extracted.
- **Clever headings.** "The Secret Sauce" tells neither readers nor engines what the section is about.
- **Walls of text.** Ten-sentence paragraphs blend several ideas into one chunk, making none of them cleanly quotable.
- **Content hidden behind interactions.** Answers that only appear after clicks, inside images or in scripts that crawlers may not render are harder to retrieve.
- **Inconsistent terminology.** Calling the same thing by three different names across a page makes it harder to match a question to an answer.

## A Rewrite Workflow for Existing Content

The fastest gains usually come from rewriting pages you already have, not publishing new ones. Pages that already rank or get traffic have proven relevance; they often just need sharper passages.

1. **Pick a page and list its questions.** Write down every question the page should answer, using phrasing from search queries, sales calls, support tickets and "People also ask" style questions.
2. **Map each question to a section.** If a question has no clear home, add a section with a heading that names it.
3. **Rewrite each section's opening.** Make the first one or two sentences a complete, direct answer.
4. **Replace pronouns with nouns** in the first sentence of every section and paragraph.
5. **Add one specific detail per section.** A step, an example, a threshold or a named source.
6. **Add a definition** for every key term the first time it appears.
7. **Convert comparisons into tables and processes into numbered lists.**
8. **Add or refresh a short summary** at the top of the page.
9. **Read each section in isolation.** Copy it into a blank document. If it does not make sense alone, revise again.

You can then check whether the rewrite worked by running the page's priority questions through the AI engines you care about over the following weeks. ### Writing Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

Great passages only get cited if retrieval systems can reach them and have a reason to trust them. Writing is one layer of a stack, and it is worth being honest about the other layers so you do not over-attribute results to wording alone.

- **Access.** The page must be crawlable and indexable. For Google AI features, Google says normal Search eligibility is the requirement. For other engines, check that their documented crawlers, such as OpenAI's OAI-SearchBot or PerplexityBot, are not blocked in robots.txt if you want to appear in their search results.
- **Relevance.** The page must actually be a strong match for the question, which is mostly a function of topic coverage and intent.
- **Trust.** Named authors, sourced claims and a consistent brand identity give engines a reason to prefer your passage over an equally clear competitor.
- **Corroboration.** Claims that are consistent with, and referenced by, other reputable sources are easier to state confidently.

Good writing amplifies all of those. It cannot replace them.

## How to Start This Week

Here is a short plan to put passage-level writing into practice immediately.

1. **Choose your five most valuable pages** for questions your customers actually ask.
2. **Rewrite the first paragraph of each** so it answers the page's main question in 40 to 70 words.
3. **Add a one-sentence definition** for the main term on each page.
4. **Fix every section opening** so it starts with a direct answer and a named subject.
5. **Add one table or numbered list** wherever a comparison or process is currently described in prose.
6. **Create a house style rule** for your team: answer first, name the subject, one idea per paragraph, no unsourced statistics.
7. **Record a baseline** by checking how AI engines currently answer your priority questions, so you can compare later.
