Entity SEO is the practice of making your brand, your people and your offerings clearly identifiable as distinct "things" that Google's Knowledge Graph and AI assistants can recognize, connect and describe correctly. Instead of optimizing for keyword strings, you make sure every reliable source agrees on who you are, what you do and how you relate to other entities.

When this works, Google shows the right knowledge panel, AI assistants describe you accurately, and your content gets associated with the topics you want to own. When it does not, you get confused with a similarly named company, AI answers state outdated facts, or you are simply invisible because no system is confident enough to mention you. This guide explains how entities work, what the Knowledge Graph is, and a practical process for building entity clarity that holds up in both Google and AI search.

## What an entity is in SEO

An **entity** is a uniquely identifiable thing: a person, organization, place, product, event or concept that can be distinguished from everything else. "Apple" the string is ambiguous. Apple the technology company, apple the fruit and Apple Records are three separate entities with different attributes and relationships.

Search engines moved toward entity understanding years ago, a shift Google described publicly as moving from "strings to things" when it introduced the Knowledge Graph in 2012. Large language models work in a related way. They do not look up a graph the same way Google does, but they build internal associations between names, attributes and topics from the text they learn from and the pages they retrieve.

Every entity has three things worth managing:

- **Identity:** the name, aliases and identifiers that pin down which thing you mean.
- **Attributes:** facts about the entity, like founding date, location, leadership, products and category.
- **Relationships:** connections to other entities, like "founded by," "located in," "subsidiary of," "known for" and "author of."

Entity SEO is the work of making all three explicit, accurate and consistent wherever machines look.

## What Google's Knowledge Graph is and what it powers

**Google's Knowledge Graph** is a large database of entities and the relationships between them, which Google uses to understand queries about named things and to display facts directly in results. The most visible output is the knowledge panel, the box that appears for many brands, people and places, but the graph also informs how Google interprets queries, disambiguates names and connects topics.

A few points about the Knowledge Graph are worth being precise about:

- There is no submission form. Google adds and updates entities based on information it finds and corroborates.
- Knowledge panels are generated automatically. Verified representatives can claim a panel and suggest edits, but Google decides what is shown.
- Entities have machine identifiers. You can see them through the public Knowledge Graph Search API, which returns IDs, types and short descriptions for matching entities.
- Being in the graph does not guarantee a panel. Many entities exist in the graph without a visible panel for every query.

For local businesses, a verified Google Business Profile establishes a place entity, which is a separate but related path. That side of the work is covered in [Local SEO in the AI Search Era](/blog/local-seo-ai-search).

## Why entity SEO matters for AI search

Entity SEO matters for AI search because answer engines can only recommend or describe a brand they can resolve with confidence. When someone asks an assistant "who are the best fractional CMOs for SaaS companies" or "what does Example Growth Co. do," the system has to identify which entities are relevant and what is true about them before it writes a word.

I see three failure modes repeatedly when I check how assistants describe a brand:

1. **Confusion.** The assistant blends your company with another one that shares a similar name, pulling in the wrong founder, location or product line.
2. **Staleness.** The assistant states facts that were true years ago, because older descriptions are still more common across the web than current ones.
3. **Absence.** The assistant does not mention you at all for topics you clearly serve, because nothing connects your entity strongly to those topics.

All three are entity problems. Fixing them is less about any one page and more about making the web's description of you consistent and well connected. This is also why entity work sits at the center of generative engine optimization, which I explain in [What Is Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)?](/blog/what-is-generative-engine-optimization).

## The signals that build entity recognition

The signals that build entity recognition fall into two groups: what you control on your own properties, and what independent sources say about you. Strong entities have both, and the facts agree.

| Signal | Where it lives | What it establishes | Control level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entity home page | Your About page or home page | The authoritative statement of who you are | Full |
| Organization / Person schema | JSON-LD on your site | Machine-readable identity, attributes, sameAs links | Full |
| Official social profiles | LinkedIn, YouTube, X, Instagram, etc. | Corroborating identity and activity | Full |
| Google Business Profile | Google | Place entity for local businesses | High |
| Wikidata item | Wikidata | Structured, public identifiers and facts | Partial (community rules apply) |
| Industry directories and associations | Third-party sites | Category membership and legitimacy | Partial |
| Press coverage and interviews | News and trade publications | Independent confirmation and topical association | Low |
| Author bylines and podcast appearances | Other people's sites | Expertise connections for people entities | Low |

The high-control signals are where you start, because they define the canonical version of you. The low-control signals are what make that canonical version believable. Earning those independent mentions is its own discipline, covered in [Brand Mentions and Digital PR for AI Visibility](/blog/brand-mentions-digital-pr-ai).

## Build your entity home first

An **entity home** is the single page on your website that serves as the authoritative description of an entity, typically your About page for an organization or a dedicated bio page for a person. Every other profile and structured-data reference should point back to it, and it should state the core facts plainly enough that a machine or a human could summarize you from that page alone.

A strong entity home includes:

- A clear first sentence that defines the entity: "Example Growth Co. is a Denver-based digital marketing agency that specializes in technical SEO and AI search visibility."
- The canonical name, plus any former names or common variations, stated explicitly.
- Founding date, headquarters or service area, and leadership with links to their own bio pages.
- What the entity does, for whom, and the topics it is known for, in plain language.
- Links out to every official profile you control.
- Organization or Person structured data with a stable `@id` and `sameAs` links to those same profiles.

That first definitional sentence matters more than it looks. It is the line you want repeated, nearly word for word, in your LinkedIn summary, your directory listings, your speaker bios and your press boilerplate. Repetition across independent sources is what turns a claim into a fact in a machine's eyes.

Here is a compact example of the structured data for an entity home. The full graph pattern with `@id` references is explained in [Schema Markup for AI Search](/blog/schema-markup-for-ai-search).

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Organization",
  "@id": "https://www.example.com/#organization",
  "name": "Example Growth Co.",
  "alternateName": ["Example Growth", "Example Growth Company"],
  "url": "https://www.example.com/",
  "description": "Example Growth Co. is a Denver-based digital marketing agency that specializes in technical SEO and AI search visibility.",
  "foundingDate": "2012",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "addressLocality": "Denver",
    "addressRegion": "CO",
    "addressCountry": "US"
  },
  "knowsAbout": ["Technical SEO", "Generative engine optimization", "Structured data"],
  "sameAs": [
    "https://www.linkedin.com/company/example-growth-co",
    "https://www.youtube.com/@examplegrowth"
  ]
}
```

The `knowsAbout` property is a useful, underused way to state topical associations explicitly. Keep it to topics you can genuinely back up with content and credentials.

## Connect your entity to topics, people and places

Connecting your entity to topics, people and places is what turns recognition into relevance. A machine may know your company exists and still not associate it with the problems you solve. Relationships close that gap.

### Topics

Topical association comes from publishing deep, well-structured content on the subjects you want to be known for, and from being mentioned alongside those subjects elsewhere. A cluster of genuinely useful pages on one theme does more than scattered posts on many. The architecture for this is covered in [Topical Authority: Content Clusters That Win Google and AI](/blog/topical-authority-guide).

On individual articles, you can name the main entities a page discusses using `about` and `mentions`:

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Article",
  "headline": "How Retrieval Works in AI Search",
  "author": { "@id": "https://www.example.com/#founder" },
  "publisher": { "@id": "https://www.example.com/#organization" },
  "about": {
    "@type": "Thing",
    "name": "Retrieval-augmented generation",
    "sameAs": "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation"
  },
  "mentions": [
    { "@type": "Organization", "name": "Google" },
    { "@type": "Thing", "name": "Knowledge graph" }
  ]
}
```

### People

People are entities too, and for service businesses they are often the strongest entities you have. Give each key person a bio page with Person schema, link it from their bylines, and make sure their LinkedIn, speaker profiles and podcast appearances describe them consistently. An expert who is clearly connected to your organization lends that organization credibility.

### Places

If location matters to your business, make the place relationship explicit everywhere: address in structured data, a consistent NAP (name, address, phone) across directories, and location pages that describe what you do in each area you serve.

## Wikidata, Wikipedia and third-party knowledge bases

Wikidata and Wikipedia are influential public knowledge bases, but they are community projects with their own rules, not marketing channels. **Wikidata** is a free, structured knowledge base where each entity has an item with a unique Q identifier and statements about its properties. Google used Freebase as an early Knowledge Graph source and, when Freebase was shut down, its data was migrated toward Wikidata, which is one reason SEOs pay attention to it.

Handle these sources carefully:

- **Notability rules apply.** Wikidata items need to meet the project's notability criteria, which generally means the entity is referenced by serious, public sources. Items created purely for promotion are often deleted.
- **Conflict of interest matters.** Wikipedia strongly discourages editing articles about yourself or your employer. If you have a genuine article, use the talk page to request corrections rather than editing directly.
- **Accuracy beats presence.** An accurate Wikidata item with good references is useful. A thin or wrong one can propagate errors.

If you do not meet notability standards yet, that is fine. Focus on the signals you control and on earning independent coverage. Entities become notable the same way people do: by being written about.

## A step-by-step entity SEO process

A reliable entity SEO process starts by auditing how machines currently see you, then fixes the canonical source, then aligns everything else to it. Here is the sequence I use.

1. **Search yourself as a machine would.** Search your brand and key people in Google, note whether a knowledge panel appears, and check what it says. Query the Knowledge Graph Search API to see whether you resolve to an entity.
2. **Ask the assistants.** Ask ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot and Claude "What is [brand]?" and "Who founded [brand]?" Record every incorrect, outdated or missing fact.
3. **Write your canonical fact sheet.** One document with the official name, aliases, one-sentence definition, founding date, location, leadership, core offerings and official profile URLs.
4. **Build or rewrite the entity home.** Put the definition and facts on one authoritative page and add Organization or Person schema with `sameAs` links.
5. **Align your owned profiles.** Update LinkedIn, social bios, Google Business Profile and directory listings so they match the fact sheet, using the same definitional sentence where the format allows.
6. **Fix legacy inconsistencies.** Old names, old addresses and former leadership on third-party sites cause staleness. Request updates where you can.
7. **Strengthen topical relationships.** Publish focused content on your core topics, attribute it to real experts, and earn mentions that place your brand next to those topics.
8. **Claim what you can.** If a knowledge panel exists, claim it and suggest corrections through Google's process.
9. **Re-check quarterly.** Repeat steps 1 and 2 and track whether descriptions become more accurate over time.

## Common entity SEO mistakes

The most common entity SEO mistakes create inconsistency, which is the opposite of what entity understanding needs.

- **Name drift.** Using "Example Growth," "Example Growth Co." and "EG Marketing" interchangeably across properties without declaring the relationship.
- **No definitional sentence.** About pages that tell a founding story for six paragraphs without ever stating plainly what the company is.
- **Orphaned people.** Experts who write the content but have no bio page, no Person schema and no link to the organization.
- **Spammy sameAs lists.** Linking dozens of low-quality directory profiles instead of the handful of authoritative ones.
- **Self-promotional Wikipedia edits.** These tend to be reverted and can draw scrutiny to your brand.
- **Ignoring rebrands.** After a name change, old descriptions dominate for a long time unless you actively update profiles and state the former name on your entity home.

## How to start entity SEO this week

You can start entity SEO this week with a few focused hours, because most of the early wins come from fixing what you already own.

1. Run the self-audit: Google search, knowledge panel check and five AI assistant prompts. Save the answers in a spreadsheet so you can compare later.
2. Draft your one-sentence entity definition and canonical fact sheet, and get it approved internally.
3. Rewrite your About page so the definition is the first sentence and the core facts are stated plainly.
4. Add or fix Organization schema on that page with a stable `@id` and accurate `sameAs` links.
5. Update your LinkedIn company page and your top three directory listings to match the fact sheet word for word.

That alone resolves a surprising share of confusion and staleness problems. The harder, slower work of building independent coverage comes next.

## The bottom line on entities and the Knowledge Graph

Search engines and AI assistants reward brands they can identify without doubt. Entity SEO is how you remove that doubt: one authoritative entity home, structured data that says the same thing, owned profiles that agree, and independent sources that confirm it. None of it is a trick, and all of it compounds, because every consistent mention makes the next one more credible.
