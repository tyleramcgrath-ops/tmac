If you are trying to work out how to choose a rehab, the chances are you are doing it at a hard moment. Maybe you have finally decided you want help, or someone you love has. Maybe a crisis has forced the question. Either way, you are suddenly being asked to make a large, expensive, deeply personal decision, often quickly, and often while being contacted by people whose job is to sell you a bed.

This guide is meant to slow things down a little. It will not tell you which program to pick, because the right place depends on you: your substance or struggle, your health, your history, your budget and the life you are going back to. What it will do is give you the questions that separate careful, accountable treatment from places that look good in photographs and not much else.

You do not need to be an expert to ask these questions. You only need to ask them calmly, write down the answers, and notice how a program responds. A good rehab will welcome the scrutiny. A poor one will often try to hurry you past it.

How to choose a rehab: start with what you actually need

Before you look at a single website, it helps to be honest about the starting point. Rehabs vary enormously, and a beautiful setting is no substitute for the right level of care. A few questions to sit with, ideally with a doctor or other clinician who knows you:

  • What are you seeking help for? Alcohol, opioids, stimulants, prescription medications, gambling, an eating disorder, depression, trauma, burnout, or several of these at once.
  • Do you need medical detox? If you drink heavily every day, or use benzodiazepines or opioids regularly, stopping suddenly may not be safe. More on this below.
  • Are there mental health conditions alongside the substance use? Anxiety, depression, PTSD, bipolar disorder and ADHD commonly occur together with addiction, and both need attention.
  • What level of structure do you need? Residential (living on site), partial hospitalisation or day programs, intensive outpatient, or regular outpatient therapy. Each suits different needs, and many people move between them over time.
  • What practical limits do you have? Work, children, caring duties, insurance, distance, and how long you can realistically step away.

In the US, many clinicians use the ASAM Criteria from the American Society of Addiction Medicine to match people to a level of care based on things like withdrawal risk, physical and mental health, readiness to change, relapse risk and home environment. You do not need to learn the framework, but it is worth asking any program how it decides what level of care you need. "Everyone does our 30-day program" is not an assessment.

The US National Institute on Drug Abuse sets out a set of principles of effective treatment that make a useful lens here. Among them: no single treatment is right for everyone, treatment needs to address the whole person and not just the drug use, and staying in treatment long enough matters. Keep those ideas in mind as you read on.

Licensing and accreditation: the non-negotiable first check

Licensing is the floor, not the ceiling. It tells you a facility is legally allowed to operate and is subject to some form of oversight. It does not tell you the care is excellent, but a program without it should be off your list.

Licensing

Licensing rules depend on where the facility is. In the US, treatment programs are generally licensed at state level, usually by a department of health or behavioral health. In England, providers of regulated health and social care, including many residential rehabs, register with the Care Quality Commission (CQC), which inspects services and publishes reports. Other countries have their own health ministries or regulators; in some, oversight of private retreats is light or patchy.

Ask directly:

  • "Which body licenses or registers you, and what is your licence or registration number?"
  • "Does the licence cover the specific services you are offering me, such as detox or residential care?"
  • "Have you had any inspections, enforcement actions or complaints upheld in recent years?"

Accreditation

Accreditation is voluntary and goes beyond the legal minimum. In the US, the most widely recognised accrediting bodies for behavioral health are The Joint Commission and CARF International. Accreditation means an outside organisation has reviewed the program against published standards. It is a good sign, though not a guarantee, and some excellent smaller programs, especially outside the US, may not hold it. If a program claims accreditation, ask which body, and check it on that body's own website rather than relying on a logo in the footer.

How to verify a licence yourself

Verifying takes ten or fifteen minutes and is one of the most protective things you can do. Do not rely on what the admissions team says, however friendly they are. Go to the source.

  1. Get the exact legal name and address. Marketing names and legal names are often different. Ask for both, and for the licence number.
  2. Look it up with the regulator. In the US, search your state's licensing agency (search the state name plus "substance use treatment licence lookup"). SAMHSA's FindTreatment.gov is a confidential locator of state-licensed facilities; being listed there does not mean SAMHSA endorses a program, but it is a sensible cross-check. In England, search the CQC website by provider name or postcode and read the latest inspection report.
  3. Check the details match. The name, address and type of service should line up with what you were told. A licence for outpatient counselling is not a licence for medical detox.
  4. Look up key clinicians. Doctors, nurses, psychologists and counsellors are usually individually licensed. Most licensing boards have a public search where you can confirm a licence is current and see whether there have been disciplinary actions.
  5. Verify accreditation on the accreditor's site. Both The Joint Commission and CARF provide ways to check accredited organisations.
  6. For programs abroad, ask which national or regional health authority oversees them, and whether the medical team holds local licences. If the answers are vague, treat that as information. Ask, too, how the program would hand your care over to clinicians at home.

On our own directory, many listings come from official public registries, and you can search all listings by location and type of care as a starting point. Even so, always confirm current licensing directly with the regulator before you commit; registrations change.

Who will actually be treating you?

A rehab is only as good as the people in it. Websites tend to feature the medical director and a smiling founder; you want to know who you will see day to day.

Questions worth asking:

  • "Is there a physician on staff, and are they trained or certified in addiction medicine or addiction psychiatry?"
  • "Is a psychiatrist available to assess and treat mental health conditions, and how often would I see them?"
  • "Are nurses on site? Around the clock or only during the day?"
  • "What qualifications do your therapists hold? Are they licensed clinicians, such as psychologists, clinical social workers, licensed professional counsellors or certified addiction counsellors?"
  • "How many hours a week will I spend in individual therapy, as opposed to groups or activities?"
  • "What is your staff-to-client ratio, and how much staff turnover have you had?"

People in recovery often work in treatment as peer supporters or recovery coaches, and that lived experience can be genuinely valuable. It should sit alongside, not replace, licensed clinical staff. If the only answer to "who provides therapy?" is "our counsellors are all in recovery themselves," keep asking.

Evidence-based therapies: what happens all day?

Ask for a sample weekly schedule. It is the clearest window into what a program actually does. Look for a core of approaches with solid research behind them, such as:

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), which helps you notice and change the thoughts and behaviours that drive use, and build skills for coping with triggers and cravings.
  • Motivational interviewing or motivational enhancement, which works with ambivalence rather than against it.
  • Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), especially for emotional regulation and for people with self-harm or intense distress.
  • Contingency management, which uses tangible rewards for meeting goals and has a strong evidence base for stimulant use in particular.
  • Family therapy or family involvement, where appropriate and safe.
  • Trauma-focused therapies delivered by trained clinicians, where trauma is part of the picture.

Medications for addiction treatment

For opioid use disorder and alcohol use disorder, there are approved medications that reduce cravings, lower the risk of relapse and, in the case of opioids, reduce the risk of overdose death. SAMHSA, NIDA and NICE all recognise these medications as part of effective care. Ask plainly: "Do you offer, or support people who take, medications for opioid or alcohol use disorder? Can I continue a medication I am already prescribed?" A program that refuses or discourages these medications on principle is not following current evidence for those conditions. Decisions about whether a medication is right for you belong with a prescriber who knows your history.

Complementary therapies

Yoga, mindfulness, nature, art, exercise and good food can make a stay more humane and help you build a life you want to protect. They are worth having. They are not, on their own, treatment for addiction. If a program's schedule is mostly massage and excursions with little structured therapy, it may be closer to a retreat than to clinical care. Our piece on holistic rehab explores what a genuine whole-person program looks like.

Medical safety: detox and dual diagnosis

Safe withdrawal is not optional

Withdrawal from some substances can be dangerous. Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal in particular can cause seizures and a life-threatening condition called delirium tremens, and should never be attempted alone if you have been using heavily or for a long time. Opioid withdrawal is rarely life-threatening in itself but is very hard to get through without support, and the drop in tolerance afterwards raises the risk of overdose. Please talk to a doctor before you stop.

When you speak to a rehab, ask:

  • "Do you provide medically supervised detox on site, or do you refer to a hospital or detox unit first?"
  • "Who assesses withdrawal risk, and how? Is a doctor involved before I arrive?"
  • "Is there 24-hour nursing during detox? What happens if there is a medical emergency, and how far is the nearest hospital?"
  • "How do you manage withdrawal symptoms with medication?"

Not every rehab needs to provide detox itself; many work well with a separate detox unit first. What matters is that there is a safe, joined-up plan and nobody is leaving you to "tough it out." You can read more in what happens in medical detox, or browse medically supervised programs.

Dual diagnosis: are both conditions treated together?

Many people who struggle with substances are also living with depression, anxiety, trauma or another mental health condition. Sometimes one came first; often they feed each other. Treating only the drinking, while leaving untreated depression in place, tends to set people up to struggle later.

Ask:

  • "Do you treat co-occurring mental health conditions, or only substance use?"
  • "Will I have a psychiatric assessment when I arrive?"
  • "Can you prescribe and manage psychiatric medication if I need it?"
  • "Are there conditions you are not equipped to treat, such as active psychosis, severe eating disorders or high suicide risk? Where would you refer me?"

An honest answer to that last question is a good sign. No program is right for everyone, and the best ones know their limits. Our guide to dual diagnosis treatment goes deeper.

Aftercare: what happens when you go home?

Leaving residential treatment is one of the most vulnerable points in recovery. You go from a protected, structured environment back to the same streets, people and pressures. A program's real quality often shows in how seriously it plans for that moment.

Ask:

  • "When does discharge planning start?" (The best answer is: soon after you arrive.)
  • "Will you help me find a therapist, doctor or outpatient program near where I live?"
  • "Do you offer step-down care, alumni groups, check-ins or online follow-up?"
  • "How do you help me plan for relapse risk, and what happens if I do relapse?"
  • "How will you involve my family or support network, if I want them involved?"

Relapse is common in many chronic conditions, and addiction is no exception. A program that treats it as a reason for shame, rather than a signal to adjust the plan, may not be the right fit. There is more in our guide to life after rehab and aftercare.

Red flags to take seriously

Most people working in treatment care deeply about the people they serve. But the industry has also attracted operators who see vulnerable people and their insurance as a revenue stream. These warning signs are worth knowing.

Patient brokering and paid referrals

Patient brokering is the practice of paying, or receiving payment, for steering someone into a particular facility. It can look like a "helpline" that seems neutral but routes every caller to the same few programs, a recruiter who approaches you in a recovery meeting or on social media, or a sober home that sends residents back into treatment repeatedly so their insurance can be billed. Brokering is illegal in many places, including under US federal law for many kinds of facility. Ask any referral service: "Are you paid by the facilities you recommend?" and ask any rehab: "Do you pay for referrals?"

"Guaranteed cure" or success-rate boasts

Addiction is a treatable condition, and many people recover. But no honest program can guarantee a cure, and dramatic success rates are rarely backed by independent, published data. If a figure is quoted, ask how it was measured: who was counted, over what period, and who did the follow-up. Vague or evasive answers tell you a lot.

Pressure tactics

"We only have one bed left." "The price goes up tomorrow." "If you don't come today, you may not survive." Urgency can be real when someone is in danger, and in that case the right response is emergency medical care, not a sales call. A legitimate program will give you time to verify their licence, talk to your doctor and read the paperwork.

Free flights, free rent and other inducements

Offers to pay for your travel, give you free housing, or waive your deductible or co-pay "as a gift" can be a sign that the real money is being made by billing your insurance, sometimes for services you do not need. Be especially cautious if someone offers to fly you across the country to a program you had never heard of an hour ago.

Other warning signs

  • Reluctance to share a licence number, the legal entity name or staff credentials.
  • No clear, itemised explanation of costs, or pressure to pay large sums up front.
  • A one-size-fits-all program length and schedule for everyone.
  • Hostility to medications for opioid or alcohol use disorder.
  • Heavy emphasis on luxury and amenities, with little detail on clinical care.
  • Policies that cut you off from family or outside contact entirely without a clear clinical reason.
  • Reviews that all sound the same, or a reputation you cannot find anywhere independent.

Touring, costs and getting it in writing

Visit in person or on a live video call

If you can visit, do. If you cannot, ask for a live video walkthrough, not a pre-recorded marketing video. You are looking for whether the place feels safe, clean, calm and genuinely staffed, and whether the people you speak to answer questions without deflecting.

During a tour or call, try to:

  • See the bedrooms, group rooms, therapy offices and medical area, not just the pool and dining room.
  • Speak to a clinician, not only admissions or sales staff.
  • Ask what a typical day looks like, hour by hour.
  • Ask about house rules, phone and visitor policies, and how disagreements or rule breaks are handled.
  • Notice how current clients seem, if you see them. Respect their privacy, but trust your sense of the atmosphere.
  • Ask what happens if you want to leave early. You are a voluntary client; you should be told clearly how that works.

Pay attention to how you feel afterwards. Calm and respected is a good sign. Rushed, flattered or vaguely pressured is worth pausing on.

Costs, insurance and an itemised quote

Costs vary enormously between countries, settings and levels of care, and headline prices often leave things out. Rather than comparing advertised numbers, ask each program for an itemised quote that shows what is included: assessment, detox, medication, psychiatric care, individual therapy hours, family sessions, testing, aftercare and any extras. Ask what happens financially if you leave early or need to stay longer.

If you have insurance, call your insurer yourself to check whether a program is in network and what you would owe, rather than relying only on the facility's verification. In the US, HealthCare.gov explains how marketplace plans cover substance use and mental health services. Our guide on how to pay for rehab covers other options.

A checklist of questions to ask any rehab

Print this, or keep it open while you call. Write down the answers and the name of the person you spoke to.

  1. What is your legal name, and which body licenses or registers you? What is the licence number?
  2. Are you accredited, and by whom?
  3. How do you assess what level of care I need?
  4. Who is on your clinical team, and what are their qualifications?
  5. How many hours of individual therapy will I have each week?
  6. Which therapies do you use, and can I see a sample weekly schedule?
  7. Do you offer or support medications for opioid or alcohol use disorder?
  8. How do you handle detox, and is there 24-hour medical cover?
  9. Do you treat co-occurring mental health conditions? What do you not treat?
  10. How do you involve family, if I want that?
  11. What does aftercare look like, and when does discharge planning start?
  12. What happens if I relapse during or after treatment?
  13. Can I have an itemised written quote? What is not included?
  14. Do you pay for referrals, or offer free travel or housing?
  15. Can I tour in person, or by live video, and speak to a clinician?

FAQ

How long should rehab last?

There is no single right length. Research summarised by NIDA suggests that staying in treatment long enough matters, and that many people benefit from a longer overall course of care than a single short stay. That course might include residential treatment followed by outpatient therapy and ongoing support. Be wary of programs that set the same length for everyone, and ask how they decide when someone is ready to step down.

Is a luxury rehab better than a standard one?

Not necessarily. Comfort, privacy and beautiful surroundings can make a stay easier, and that has value. But the quality of treatment comes from licensed clinicians, evidence-based therapy, medical safety and good aftercare planning, not thread counts. A modest program with excellent staff can serve you far better than an expensive one with thin clinical care. Judge every program by the same questions.

How can I tell if a rehab helpline is neutral?

Ask whether the service is owned by, or paid by, any treatment facility, and whether it receives money for referrals. Check whether the site names the company behind it. Government services such as the SAMHSA National Helpline and FindTreatment.gov in the US are free and do not sell placements. If a "helpline" only ever recommends one or two programs, treat its advice with caution.

Should I choose a rehab close to home or far away?

Both can work. Staying close makes family involvement and a smooth handover to local aftercare easier, and you can keep links with your own doctor. Travelling further can offer distance from triggers and access to a specialist program. If you go far, ask how the program will connect you with support where you live, so you are not starting from scratch when you return.

What if the person who needs rehab does not want to go?

This is very common and very painful. Pressure and ultimatums often backfire, while calm, consistent conversations and approaches such as CRAFT, a family-focused method, can help. Looking after your own wellbeing matters too. If someone is in immediate danger, call emergency services. Speaking to a family support group or counsellor yourself can help.

Getting help now

If you or someone you know is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. In the US, call or text 988 for the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call the SAMHSA National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7) for treatment referrals and information. In the UK, call NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, find a local line at findahelpline.com.
