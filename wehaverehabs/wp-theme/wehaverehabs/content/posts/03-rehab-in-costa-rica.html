For many people, the idea of rehab in Costa Rica begins with a picture: green hills, warm rain, the sound of the ocean somewhere below. It is an appealing image, and for some people the move to a quieter, gentler place really does help them focus on recovery in a way they never managed at home. But a beautiful setting is not the same thing as good care, and the decision deserves more than a glance at a photo gallery.

This guide walks you through why people choose Costa Rica, what kinds of programs you will find there, how the main regions differ, the practical side of travel, and, most importantly, how to check that a program is properly run. We will also be honest about the downsides, because going abroad for treatment is not right for everyone.

If you want to browse listings as you read, our Costa Rica destination page brings together the retreats and programs we know about in one place. Use it as a starting point for your own research, not as the final word.

Why people consider rehab in Costa Rica

People come to Costa Rica for treatment for a handful of recurring reasons. None of them are magic, but each can matter.

Nature and a slower pace

Costa Rica is known for its forests, volcanoes, rivers and long stretches of coastline, and a large share of the country is set aside as protected land. Many programs make the most of this, building their days around walks, time outdoors, swimming, gardening or simply sitting somewhere quiet. For someone whose life has been loud, crowded and screen-filled, that change of rhythm can make it easier to sleep, think and talk honestly.

A mild, warm climate

Much of the country is warm year round, with cooler temperatures at higher elevations. It is easy to spend time outside, which suits programs that use movement, outdoor therapy or simple daily routines in the open air. The rhythm of the year is shaped more by wet and dry seasons than by winter and summer, which is worth knowing before you pack.

Distance from familiar triggers

One of the strongest reasons people give is distance. Being far from the bar on the corner, the friends you used with, the office that kept you working until midnight, or the family tensions that wear you down can create breathing room. You are, at least for a while, out of reach of many of the people and places tied to old habits.

That distance is a double-edged thing, though. The same triggers will usually still be waiting when you return, which is why aftercare planning (covered below) matters so much.

Privacy

Some people simply want to step away without colleagues or neighbours knowing. Treatment far from home can feel more private, although you should still ask any program how it handles your personal and medical information.

Types of programs: wellness retreats vs clinical programs

This is the single most important distinction to understand. In Costa Rica, as in many popular destinations, the word "rehab" is used loosely. It can describe anything from a yoga retreat with a sobriety theme to a medically staffed residential program. These are not the same, and they are not interchangeable.

Wellness and holistic retreats

Wellness retreats focus on rest, reflection, nutrition, movement and often mindfulness or meditation. They may offer counselling, coaching or group sharing, and they can be a lovely place to reset after burnout, grief or a hard stretch of life. Many people find them helpful for stress, exhaustion or early, mild concerns about drinking. You can read more about this style of care in our guide to holistic rehab and browse retreat-style listings.

What retreats usually do not offer is medical care. If a retreat has no doctor or nurse on site, it is not the right place for anyone who needs detox, has a serious mental health condition, or is at risk of complicated withdrawal.

Clinical and medically supervised programs

Clinical programs are built around structured, evidence-based treatment: assessments, individual therapy, group therapy, family work, relapse-prevention planning and, where needed, psychiatric care. Some also provide medically supervised detox. Many Costa Rican clinical programs blend this with holistic elements such as yoga, nature time or nutrition, which can be a good combination if the clinical core is solid. See our listings for clinical programs and medically supervised care.

Why the difference matters for safety

Withdrawal from alcohol and from benzodiazepines can be dangerous, and in some cases life-threatening. It should never be attempted alone or in a setting without medical oversight. If you are physically dependent on either, or on opioids or other substances, you need a program with qualified medical staff who can assess you and supervise withdrawal, or you need to complete detox under medical care before you travel. Our explainer on what happens in medical detox covers this in more detail.

Similarly, if you are living with a mental health condition alongside substance use, look for a program that treats both together. This is often called dual diagnosis care.

Regions at a glance

Costa Rica is a small country, but its regions feel quite different. Here is a general overview to help you picture where a program sits. Conditions vary from place to place, so always ask the program itself about access, weather and nearby medical facilities.

The Central Valley

The Central Valley is the country's most populated area and includes the capital, San José, along with surrounding towns and hillsides. Programs here tend to be closer to larger hospitals, specialist doctors and the main international airport. The climate is generally milder than on the coasts. If medical access is a priority for you, this region is often worth a close look.

Guanacaste and the Pacific coast

The northwest province of Guanacaste and the wider Pacific coast are known for beaches, sunshine and a distinct dry season. This area has its own international airport, which can make travel simpler. Programs here often lean into ocean views, surfing, swimming and outdoor activity. Some locations are fairly remote, so ask how far the nearest hospital is and how emergencies are handled.

The Caribbean side

The Caribbean coast has a different feel: a strong Afro-Caribbean cultural heritage, lush rainforest and a wetter climate. It is generally quieter and less developed for large-scale tourism than parts of the Pacific side. Travel times from the main airport can be longer, which suits some people and not others.

The mountains and cloud forests

Higher-elevation areas, including cloud forest regions, are cooler, misty and very green. They can feel deeply peaceful and secluded. They can also be harder to reach, with winding roads and changeable weather. Seclusion is wonderful until you need a specialist, so weigh it carefully against your health needs.

Travel practicalities

Getting to treatment should be one of the least stressful parts of the process. A little planning helps.

Flights and airports

Most international travellers arrive through one of two airports: Juan Santamaría International Airport (SJO), near San José in the Central Valley, or Daniel Oduber Quirós International Airport (LIR), near Liberia in Guanacaste. Which one makes sense depends on where your program is. Many programs offer, or can arrange, an airport pickup. Ask whether a staff member will meet you, because arriving alone and unsure of the next step can be hard, especially in early recovery.

Entry rules change, so check official sources

Passport validity rules, length-of-stay limits, proof of onward travel and any health requirements can change, and they differ by nationality. Do not rely on a program's website or a forum post for this. Check the current advice from your own government and from Costa Rican authorities before you book. For example, US citizens can check the US State Department's Costa Rica page, and UK travellers can check the UK government's foreign travel advice.

Medications

If you take prescribed medication, talk to your doctor before you go. Bring enough for the journey, keep it in its original packaging with a copy of the prescription, and check whether any of your medicines are restricted at your destination. Tell the program exactly what you take so their clinicians can plan safely. Never stop or change a medication on your own because you are travelling.

Travelling in early recovery

Airports and flights can be stressful, and alcohol is often easy to find while travelling. If you are worried about the journey, consider asking a trusted friend or family member to travel with you, or ask the program whether they offer an escort service. If you are in acute withdrawal, travelling is not a good idea at all; get medical help first.

How to vet a program in Costa Rica

This is where most of your effort should go. A well-made website and gorgeous photographs tell you nothing about clinical quality. The questions below are the ones we would want answered before trusting anyone with our own care or the care of someone we love. Our broader guide on how to choose a rehab goes deeper on several of them.

Permits and health ministry authorisation

Health services in Costa Rica fall under the oversight of the country's Ministry of Health, the Ministerio de Salud. Ask the program directly which operating permits and health ministry authorisation they hold, ask for the details in writing, and then verify them independently rather than taking a logo on a website at face value. If a program is vague, defensive or says permits do not apply to them, treat that as a warning sign.

Staff and qualifications

Ask who actually delivers the care. Are there licensed physicians, psychiatrists, psychologists, nurses and addiction counsellors? Where did they train, and where are they licensed? Is a doctor on site, on call or only reachable by phone? How many clients are there per therapist? A program that relies mainly on coaches or people in recovery, without clinical oversight, may be fine for a wellness stay but is not a substitute for treatment.

Questions checklist

Copy this list into your notes and work through it on a call with each program you are considering:

  • Which operating permits and Ministerio de Salud authorisation do you hold, and can you send me the details so I can verify them?
  • Do you provide medically supervised detox on site? If not, where and how is it arranged?
  • Who is on your clinical team, what are their qualifications, and where are they licensed?
  • Is there a doctor or nurse on site around the clock? What happens in a medical or psychiatric emergency at night?
  • How far is the nearest hospital, and how would I get there?
  • Which therapies do you use, and how is my treatment plan built and reviewed?
  • Can you treat co-occurring mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety or trauma?
  • How many clients do you have at a time, and how many sessions of individual therapy will I get each week?
  • What language are sessions held in, and are clinicians fluent in my language?
  • What exactly is included in the fee? Can you give me an itemised quote, including any extra costs?
  • What is your refund policy if I leave early or need to be transferred?
  • How do you involve family, and how do you protect my privacy?
  • What does aftercare look like, and will you help me connect with support at home before I leave?

Warning signs

  • Guarantees of a "cure" or success rates that sound too good to be true, especially without a clear source.
  • Pressure to book and pay immediately, or heavy discounts that expire today.
  • Reluctance to name staff or share credentials.
  • No clear answer about emergencies or medical care.
  • Unproven or risky treatments presented as the main therapy.
  • Payment only through unusual channels, with no contract.

Language and culture

Spanish is the official language of Costa Rica. Many programs that serve international clients run their therapy entirely in English, and some offer other languages too. Even so, it is worth confirming that the therapists who will work with you are genuinely fluent in your language. Therapy depends on nuance, and talking about painful experiences is hard enough without searching for words.

Outside the program, you may find that English is widely spoken in tourist areas and less so elsewhere. Some people enjoy picking up a little Spanish during their stay; for others, the language gap adds to a feeling of being far from home. Neither reaction is wrong. Simply think about which is more likely for you.

Costa Rica also has its own culture and rhythms, including the well-known phrase "pura vida," often used as a greeting and a way of life. Many visitors find the warmth and slower pace restorative. It is still a different country with its own norms, so a little humility and curiosity go a long way.

Paying for treatment abroad: insurance and costs

Costs vary enormously between programs, from modest retreats to high-end clinical residences. Rather than relying on headline prices, ask each program for an itemised quote that sets out exactly what is included: accommodation, meals, therapy sessions, medical care, detox, medication, activities, airport transfers and any extras.

Insurance usually will not cover it

Most health insurance plans do not cover treatment at facilities outside your home country, and many do not cover wellness retreats at all. A few international or expatriate plans may offer some cover, but you should never assume. Before you book, call your insurer, ask specifically about out-of-network and international treatment for substance use or mental health, and get the answer in writing.

Some programs offer documentation that you can submit for possible reimbursement, but reimbursement is never guaranteed. Our guide on how to pay for rehab covers the options in more depth.

Travel insurance

Standard travel insurance often excludes pre-existing conditions and treatment-related travel. If you buy a policy, read the exclusions carefully and be honest about why you are travelling. Being uninsured for an emergency abroad can be very expensive.

Don't forget the hidden costs

  • Flights, and possibly flights for a family member who joins for a family programme.
  • Time away from work, and any loss of income.
  • Childcare, pet care or house-sitting while you are away.
  • Aftercare at home, such as therapy or an outpatient program, which may need separate funding.

Aftercare planning back home

Recovery does not end when the plane lands. The research on addiction is clear that it is a chronic, treatable condition and that ongoing support matters; the National Institute on Drug Abuse's principles of effective treatment note that staying in treatment long enough, and continuing care afterwards, is important for good outcomes. When you get treatment abroad, the bridge back to everyday life needs extra attention.

What a good aftercare plan includes

  • A named therapist or clinician at home, ideally booked before you leave Costa Rica, so there is no gap.
  • A step-down option, such as an intensive outpatient or day program, if you need more structure for a while. Our guide to inpatient vs outpatient rehab explains how these levels fit together.
  • Peer support, such as mutual-help groups, in person or online.
  • A written relapse-prevention plan that names your triggers, early warning signs and who you will call.
  • Medical follow-up, especially if you were started on any medication during treatment.
  • A plan for home itself: removing alcohol or drugs from the house, talking honestly with family, and thinking ahead about work, social events and holidays.

Ask every program how they handle discharge. The best ones start planning aftercare from the first week and will help you connect with services in your own country. For more ideas, see our article on life after rehab and aftercare.

Online continuing care

Some Costa Rican programs offer online check-ins, alumni groups or video therapy after you go home. This can be a helpful thread of continuity, but it should add to, not replace, local support you can reach in person.

The honest downsides

We would not be doing our job if we only told you the good parts. Going abroad for treatment has real drawbacks, and they deserve a fair hearing.

  • The "geographic cure" trap. Changing your surroundings can help, but it does not change the underlying condition. Without solid therapy and aftercare, old patterns often return once you are home.
  • Distance from family. Family involvement can strengthen recovery. It is harder to do family sessions or visits when everyone is thousands of miles away.
  • Harder continuity of care. Your home doctor and the program may not easily share records or plans, and the handover is often the weakest point.
  • Variable regulation and quality. Standards differ between countries, and some operators market themselves well without matching clinical quality. You must do your own verification.
  • Limited legal recourse. If something goes wrong, resolving a dispute or complaint across borders can be slow and complicated.
  • Cost without insurance. Paying out of pocket can place real strain on finances, which can itself become a source of stress after treatment.
  • Medical access in remote areas. The most beautiful, secluded spots may be a long way from a hospital.
  • Re-entry shock. Coming back from a calm, supported environment to ordinary life can feel abrupt. Plan for it.

None of these rule Costa Rica out. They simply mean that the decision should be made with clear eyes, ideally after a conversation with a clinician who knows your history. If you are weighing several countries, our guide to rehab abroad compares the wider considerations.

Is rehab in Costa Rica right for you?

A program in Costa Rica may be a good fit if you:

  • Have been assessed by a clinician and know what level of care you need.
  • Feel that time away from your usual environment would genuinely help you focus.
  • Have found a program that can show you verifiable permits, qualified staff and a clear medical and emergency plan.
  • Can afford the cost without putting yourself or your family in serious financial difficulty.
  • Have, or can build, a solid aftercare plan at home.

It may be less suitable if you:

  • Need urgent medical detox or are in crisis right now.
  • Have complex medical needs that require specialist care close at hand.
  • Rely on family involvement and they cannot travel or join remotely.
  • Would find the distance, language or travel overwhelming rather than freeing.
  • Have good, accessible treatment options closer to home that you have not yet explored.

There is no shame in choosing local treatment. Many people do very well in programs a short drive from home, and some find that proximity to their real life is exactly what helps change last. You can search all of our listings, at home and abroad, from the sanctuaries directory.

FAQ

Is rehab in Costa Rica safe?

It can be, but safety depends on the specific program, not the country. Look for a program that can show you verifiable health ministry authorisation, qualified medical and clinical staff, a clear emergency plan and reasonable access to a hospital. If you need detox, make sure it is medically supervised. Avoid any program that is vague about credentials or pressures you to book quickly.

Do Costa Rican rehab programs speak English?

Many programs that serve international clients run therapy in English, and some also offer other languages. Spanish is the official language of the country, though, so do not assume. Ask specifically whether your therapists and doctors are fluent in your language, because therapy relies heavily on being able to express yourself clearly and be understood in return.

Will my health insurance pay for rehab in Costa Rica?

Usually not. Most health insurance plans do not cover treatment outside your home country, and many do not cover wellness retreats at all. Some international plans may offer partial cover. Call your insurer before booking, ask about international and out-of-network treatment specifically, and get any answer in writing. Ask programs for an itemised quote so you understand the full cost.

Can I detox in Costa Rica?

Some clinical programs offer medically supervised detox, but many retreats do not. Withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines can be dangerous and should always happen under medical supervision, never alone. If a program cannot provide on-site medical detox, you may need to complete detox before travelling. Discuss your situation with a doctor before you make plans.

How long do people usually stay?

Length of stay varies by program and by person. Some retreats offer short stays, while clinical residential programs often run for several weeks. What matters most is that the length matches your needs, as assessed by a clinician, and that there is a clear plan for continuing care once you return home, so progress made abroad is not lost.

How do I verify a program's credentials?

Ask the program which operating permits and Ministerio de Salud authorisation they hold, and request the details in writing. Then verify them independently rather than trusting a badge on a website. Also ask where each clinician is licensed and check those licences where you can. A reputable program will answer openly and will not be offended by the question.

Getting help now

If you or someone you love is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. In the US, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call the free, confidential SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357, available 24/7. In the UK, call NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, find a local service at findahelpline.com.
