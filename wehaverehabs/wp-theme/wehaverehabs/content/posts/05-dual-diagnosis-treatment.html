If you have ever wondered whether your drinking is "really" the problem, or whether it is the anxiety underneath, you are not alone. Many people arrive at treatment carrying more than one thing at once. Dual diagnosis treatment is the name for care that looks after both a mental health condition and a substance use condition at the same time, by the same team, as part of one plan.

That sounds simple, but it hasn't always been how care worked. For a long time, people were told to get sober first and deal with their depression later, or to stabilise their mood before anyone would talk about their drinking. Many fell through the gap between two systems that didn't talk to each other.

This guide explains what co-occurring conditions are, why integrated care matters, what a good program actually does day to day, and the questions worth asking before you commit. It is written for you whether you are looking for yourself or for someone you love.

What "dual diagnosis" actually means

"Dual diagnosis" is an older, informal term. Clinicians and public health agencies now more often say co-occurring disorders or co-occurring conditions. All of these describe the same situation: a person has a substance use disorder (involving alcohol, opioids, stimulants, cannabis, sedatives or other drugs) and, at the same time, at least one mental health condition such as depression, an anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder or bipolar disorder.

The word "dual" can be a little misleading. Some people live with three or four overlapping conditions, and that is not unusual. What matters is not the count, but the fact that the conditions interact. Sleep, mood, stress, physical health, relationships and substance use all pull on one another, and a plan that treats only one strand tends to leave the rest tangled.

The US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) describes co-occurring disorders as common and notes that people with one condition are more likely to experience the other. You can read its overview on the SAMHSA co-occurring disorders page. The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) likewise explains that substance use disorders and other mental illnesses frequently occur together, and summarises the research on its co-occurring disorders and health conditions page.

Which came first?

People often want to know which condition "caused" the other. Sometimes there is a clear story: someone began drinking to quiet panic attacks, or began using stimulants to cope with exhaustion. Sometimes substance use appears to bring on or worsen mental health symptoms. And sometimes both share deeper roots, such as genetics, early adversity, trauma or chronic stress. NIDA describes these overlapping risk factors and pathways in its research summaries.

For your recovery, the chicken-and-egg question is usually less important than it feels. A good team will be curious about your history, but it will not make you wait for an answer before helping with both conditions together.

Why integrated dual diagnosis treatment matters

Integrated care means your mental health and your substance use are assessed, planned for and treated together, by clinicians who share information and work towards the same goals. It is the opposite of "sequential" care (one problem first, then the other) or "parallel" care (two separate services that may never compare notes).

SAMHSA and NIDA both point to integrated treatment as the preferred approach for people with co-occurring conditions. The reasoning is practical and intuitive:

  • Symptoms overlap. Withdrawal can look like anxiety. Heavy drinking can look like depression. Stimulant use can disturb sleep and mood in ways that resemble other conditions. A team that sees the whole picture is better placed to tell these apart over time.
  • Untreated symptoms drive relapse. If the panic, the low mood or the flashbacks are still there once the substance is gone, the old way of coping is still very tempting.
  • Medications need joined-up thinking. Psychiatric medications and medications for substance use disorders should be chosen and reviewed with each other in mind.
  • One plan is easier to follow. Fewer appointments in fewer places, one set of goals and one team that knows your story means less is lost in translation.

None of this means that a program which focuses mainly on addiction can't help someone with mild anxiety, or that a mental health clinic can't support someone cutting down their drinking. It does mean that when both conditions are significant, it is worth seeking a program built to treat both. You can browse a wider range of options on our mental health listings.

Common pairings in co-occurring conditions

Any mental health condition can occur alongside any substance use disorder. A few pairings come up often enough that it helps to understand them. We describe them here in general terms, without figures; if you want the research numbers, the SAMHSA and NIDA pages linked above are the place to start.

Depression and substance use

Low mood, loss of interest, poor sleep and feelings of hopelessness can make alcohol or other drugs feel like relief, at least at first. Over time, alcohol in particular is a depressant and can deepen low mood. Integrated care looks at both: treating the depression with therapy and, where appropriate, medication, while supporting changes to substance use. If you are struggling with thoughts of suicide, please contact a crisis line now; details are at the end of this article.

Anxiety disorders and substance use

Generalised anxiety, social anxiety and panic disorder are often paired with alcohol, cannabis or sedative use, because these substances can briefly dull anxious feelings. The rebound can then make anxiety worse. Careful assessment matters here, because some medications sometimes used for anxiety carry their own risks for people with a substance use history. A good program will talk you through these trade-offs rather than simply prescribing.

PTSD and trauma

Many people in addiction treatment have lived through trauma. Substances can become a way to manage intrusive memories, hypervigilance, nightmares or numbness. Trauma-informed care, which we describe below, is essential, and some programs also offer trauma-focused therapies once a person is stable enough. You can explore specialist options on our trauma and PTSD page.

Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder involves episodes of depression and, at other times, periods of elevated or irritable mood with changes in energy and sleep. Substance use can complicate diagnosis and can interfere with mood stability. People with bipolar disorder generally benefit from close psychiatric oversight and consistent medication management, which is one reason integrated programs with an on-site or closely linked psychiatrist are so valuable.

ADHD

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder often continues into adulthood and can go undiagnosed for years. Some adults find that substances seem to help them focus or calm down; others find impulsivity makes it harder to stop. Treating ADHD in someone with a substance use history requires thought, including careful choices about medication, and a good program will discuss these options openly rather than ignoring the ADHD altogether.

Other combinations

Eating disorders, personality disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, psychosis and chronic pain are among the other conditions that can occur alongside substance use. Not every program is equipped for every combination, which is why the questions later in this guide are worth working through. If you live with one of these, ask any program specifically how it would support you.

What integrated care looks like day to day

Program brochures can blur together. Here is what dual diagnosis treatment usually involves in practice, so you know what to look for and what to expect.

1. A thorough assessment at the start

Good care begins with a careful intake. Expect questions about your substance use (what, how much, how often, and what happens when you stop), your mental health history, past diagnoses and treatments, current medications, physical health, sleep, trauma, family history, and your living situation. Many programs use standardised screening tools alongside conversation.

A psychiatric assessment by a psychiatrist, psychiatric nurse practitioner or similarly qualified clinician should be part of this, not an optional extra. Some diagnoses can only be confirmed with confidence once acute withdrawal has passed, so an honest team will tell you that its first impressions may be refined over the coming weeks.

2. Safe withdrawal management, if needed

If you are physically dependent on alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids, the first step may be medically supervised withdrawal. Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal in particular can be dangerous and should not be attempted alone. Our guide to what happens in medical detox explains this stage in more detail.

3. Medication management

Medication is not right for everyone, but for many people it is an important part of recovery. In an integrated program, one prescriber or a closely coordinated team looks after both psychiatric medication and any medication for a substance use disorder. That might include reviewing medications you already take, starting new ones where appropriate, and watching carefully for side effects and interactions.

For opioid use disorder and alcohol use disorder, there are approved medications that can support recovery; SAMHSA's medications for substance use disorders pages explain the options. A program should be willing to discuss these, rather than dismissing them on principle. Decisions about any specific medication, and any dose, belong with your prescriber.

4. Individual therapy

Most integrated programs offer regular one-to-one therapy. Common approaches include cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) skills, motivational interviewing and acceptance-based therapies. The aim is twofold: to understand how your mental health and substance use feed one another, and to build practical skills for managing cravings, difficult emotions and stressful situations.

5. Trauma-informed care throughout

Trauma-informed care is not a single therapy; it is a way of running a whole program. It means staff assume that many clients may have a trauma history, and they design the environment and their interactions to feel safe, predictable and respectful. In practice that looks like explaining what is happening and why, offering choices wherever possible, avoiding shaming or confrontational styles, and paying attention to things like privacy, room arrangements and how searches or check-ins are handled.

Where appropriate, a program may also offer trauma-focused therapies once you are stable. A careful team will pace this work so that you are not overwhelmed early in recovery.

6. Group work and peer support

Groups are a core part of most programs. Some are educational (learning how depression and alcohol interact, for example); others are therapy or skills groups; others are peer-led. Hearing from people with similar experiences can be one of the most powerful parts of treatment, and integrated programs often run groups specifically for people managing both mental health and substance use.

7. Whole-person and holistic support

Many programs add exercise, nutrition support, mindfulness, yoga, creative therapies or time in nature. These can support sleep, mood and stress, and they often make a stay feel more humane. They work best alongside evidence-based clinical care, not instead of it. When you are comparing programs, ask what proportion of the week is clinical and what is complementary.

8. Family involvement

With your consent, many programs invite family or close supporters into the process through education sessions or family therapy. This can help the people around you understand both conditions and learn how to support you without taking on the role of therapist.

A sample day

Every program is different, but a weekday in a residential dual diagnosis program might look something like this:

  • Morning: wake, breakfast, a short check-in with nursing staff and any medications.
  • Mid-morning: a skills or therapy group, such as managing cravings or emotional regulation.
  • Late morning: individual therapy or a psychiatric review, depending on the day.
  • Afternoon: lunch, then an educational session, movement, or a creative or outdoor activity.
  • Evening: dinner, a peer support meeting or reflective group, and quiet time.

Outpatient and intensive outpatient programs fit similar ingredients around work and home life. The right setting depends on how severe your symptoms are, how safe home feels, and what support you have around you.

Signs a program genuinely offers integrated care

"Dual diagnosis" appears on a great many websites. Some programs truly integrate care; others offer addiction treatment with occasional visits from a psychiatrist. A few signs of the real thing:

  • Licensed mental health professionals and addiction specialists are part of the same core team, not distant contractors.
  • A psychiatric assessment happens early, and psychiatric follow-up is regular and scheduled.
  • Your treatment plan names both conditions and sets goals for each.
  • Staff meet to discuss your care together and can explain how decisions are made.
  • The program describes the types of conditions it can and cannot treat, and refers on when something is outside its scope.
  • Discharge planning starts early and includes mental health follow-up, not only addiction support.

It is also worth checking licensing and accreditation for the country you are considering. Our directory draws on public registries where they exist, but a listing is not an endorsement, and you should always verify credentials directly with the provider and the relevant regulator.

Questions to ask a dual diagnosis program

Bring this checklist to any call with an admissions team. A trustworthy program will welcome the questions and answer them plainly.

  • Do you treat co-occurring mental health and substance use conditions together, by the same team?
  • Which mental health conditions do you have experience treating, and which would you refer elsewhere?
  • Who carries out the psychiatric assessment, and how soon after admission?
  • How often will I see a psychiatrist or psychiatric prescriber during my stay?
  • How do you approach medication, including medications for opioid or alcohol use disorder?
  • What therapies do you use, and what qualifications do your therapists hold?
  • Is your program trauma-informed? What does that look like in practice?
  • Can you manage withdrawal on site, or do you arrange medical detox elsewhere first?
  • How do you handle a mental health crisis during treatment?
  • What is the staff-to-client ratio, and is there nursing or medical cover at night?
  • How are families involved, and what are the rules on privacy and consent?
  • What does aftercare planning include, and do you help arrange ongoing psychiatric care?
  • What is included in the fee, and can you give me an itemised quote?

Aftercare: the part that keeps recovery going

For people with co-occurring conditions, leaving a program is not the end of treatment. It is a change of setting. Both mental health and substance use conditions tend to be long-term, and many people find that their needs shift over months and years. A good aftercare plan is built with you before you leave, and it usually includes several of the following:

  1. Ongoing psychiatric care. A named prescriber who will continue your medication management, with an appointment booked before discharge if possible.
  2. Regular therapy. Continuing individual therapy, ideally with someone experienced in both mental health and addiction.
  3. A step-down program. Partial hospitalisation, intensive outpatient or standard outpatient care to bridge the gap between residential treatment and everyday life.
  4. Peer support. Mutual-help groups, including groups designed for people with co-occurring conditions, and recovery communities online or in person.
  5. A relapse and crisis plan. Early warning signs for both conditions, who to call, and what to do if things get harder.
  6. Practical foundations. Stable housing, routines that protect sleep, work or study arrangements, and connection with people who support your recovery.

A setback in one area doesn't erase progress in the other. If your mood dips or you use again, it is information, not failure, and it is a reason to reach back out to your team. Our guide to life after rehab and aftercare goes into more depth.

Finding dual diagnosis treatment

When you start searching, it helps to filter by both the kind of care you need and the setting you prefer. Clinical programs with psychiatric staff are often the right fit for moderate to severe co-occurring conditions; quieter retreat-style settings may suit people who are already stable and want to deepen their recovery. You can browse all listings on our sanctuaries search, or narrow to clinical programs.

In the US, SAMHSA runs FindTreatment.gov and a free, confidential National Helpline that can point you to local services. Outside the US, your family doctor or national health service is often the best first step for a referral. Wherever you are, take your time to ask questions, and trust your instincts about whether a team truly listens.

FAQ

What is dual diagnosis treatment?

Dual diagnosis treatment is care for people who have both a substance use disorder and a mental health condition, such as depression, anxiety, PTSD or bipolar disorder. In an integrated model, one coordinated team assesses and treats both conditions at the same time, with a single plan, rather than asking you to resolve one before getting help for the other. It usually combines psychiatric care, therapy, peer support and aftercare planning.

Is it better to treat addiction first and mental health later?

Public health agencies such as SAMHSA and NIDA favour integrated treatment, where both conditions are addressed together. The two often interact, so untreated mental health symptoms can make relapse more likely, and ongoing substance use can make mental health symptoms harder to treat. That said, if you are physically dependent on a substance, safe medical withdrawal may need to happen at the start, while mental health support continues alongside it.

How long does dual diagnosis treatment take?

There is no single answer. Some people spend several weeks in residential care followed by months of outpatient support; others are treated entirely as outpatients. Because co-occurring conditions are often long-term, many people continue some form of therapy, psychiatric follow-up or peer support for a long time after a formal program ends. A good team will tailor the length of care to your progress, not to a fixed package.

Will I have to take medication?

Not necessarily. Medication is one tool among several, and the decision should be made with a qualified prescriber who understands your full history. For some conditions, such as bipolar disorder, medication is often an important part of care. For opioid or alcohol use disorder, approved medications can also support recovery. You should always be able to ask questions, understand the options and share in the decision.

Can a holistic retreat treat dual diagnosis?

Some holistic programs employ psychiatrists, licensed therapists and medical staff and can offer genuinely integrated care. Others focus on wellbeing activities and are better suited to people who are already stable. Ask directly who carries out psychiatric assessments, how medication is managed and what happens in a crisis. Holistic practices can be a valuable addition, but they work best alongside evidence-based clinical treatment.

Getting help now

If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call the SAMHSA National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). In the UK, call NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, find a local helpline at findahelpline.com.
