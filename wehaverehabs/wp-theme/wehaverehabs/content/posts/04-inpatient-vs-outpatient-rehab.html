When people first look into treatment, the question of inpatient vs outpatient rehab tends to come up almost immediately. Do you need to go away and live somewhere for a while, or can you get help while still sleeping in your own bed, going to work and seeing your family? It is a fair question, and the honest answer is that it depends on you: your health, your safety, your home life and what you have already tried.

The good news is that you do not have to work it out alone, and you do not have to get it perfectly right the first time. Treatment is not a single door. It is more like a staircase, with several levels of support, and many people move up or down that staircase as their needs change.

This guide explains each level of care in plain language, introduces the framework clinicians often use to match people to the right level, and ends with a checklist you can use when you talk to a doctor or a program.

The levels of care, from most to least intensive

"Inpatient" and "outpatient" are useful shorthand, but in practice there are several distinct levels. Different countries and programs use slightly different names, so it helps to focus on what actually happens at each level rather than the label.

Medically managed inpatient care

This is the most intensive level. It usually takes place in a hospital or a specialist unit with doctors and nurses available around the clock. It is meant for people whose physical or mental health needs close medical attention: for example, someone at risk of severe or complicated withdrawal, someone with serious medical problems alongside substance use, or someone in a mental health crisis. Stays at this level are often short and focused on getting a person medically stable.

Withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines in particular can be dangerous, and it should never be attempted alone. If you are physically dependent, talk to a doctor about supervised withdrawal. Our explainer on what happens in medical detox walks through what to expect, and you can browse medically supervised programs in our directory.

Residential treatment

Residential treatment is what most people picture when they hear "rehab." You live at the program for a period of time, usually several weeks, and your days are structured around therapy, groups, education, healthy routines and rest. Staff are present at all times, although the level of on-site medical care varies a great deal between programs. Some residential settings are closely medically monitored; others are more like a therapeutic community or a quiet retreat.

Partial hospitalisation and day programs

In a partial hospitalisation or day program, you attend treatment for much of the day, often most weekdays, and go home (or to supportive housing) in the evening. It offers a lot of structure and therapy time without an overnight stay. Day programs are often used as a step down from residential care, or as a step up for someone for whom standard outpatient care is not enough.

Intensive outpatient programs (IOP)

Intensive outpatient programs involve several sessions a week, often a few hours at a time, usually a mix of group and individual therapy. Many run in the evenings or at flexible times so people can keep working or studying. IOP suits people who are medically stable and have a reasonably safe home environment but still need more than a weekly appointment.

Standard outpatient care

Standard outpatient care usually means regular sessions with a therapist, counsellor, doctor or psychiatrist, often weekly or every couple of weeks. It might include medication management, talking therapy and peer support. It is the most flexible and least disruptive level, and it is often where people continue their recovery for the long term after more intensive treatment.

The ASAM Criteria, explained simply

In the United States and in many programs elsewhere, clinicians use a framework called the ASAM Criteria, developed by the American Society of Addiction Medicine, to help decide which level of care fits a person best. You do not need to memorise it, but knowing the basics will help you understand what an assessor is doing and why they might recommend one setting over another.

The levels, in broad strokes

The ASAM Criteria describe a continuum of care in numbered levels. In simple terms:

  • Level 1: outpatient services. Regular appointments, from standard counselling through to medically managed outpatient care.
  • Level 2: intensive outpatient and higher-intensity outpatient services. More hours each week, including what has traditionally been called partial hospitalisation or day treatment.
  • Level 3: residential and inpatient services. Living at a facility, with different sub-levels depending on how intense the clinical and medical support is.
  • Level 4: medically managed intensive inpatient services. Hospital-level care with round-the-clock medical and nursing support.

Within each level, decimal sub-levels (such as 2.1 or 3.5) describe finer differences in intensity. The framework is periodically updated, and the most recent edition renamed and reorganised some of these sub-levels, so the exact labels you see may vary depending on which edition a program uses. The overall idea of a ladder from least to most intensive stays the same.

The dimensions an assessor looks at

Rather than placing people by diagnosis alone, the ASAM approach looks at the whole person across several areas, or dimensions. In everyday language, an assessor will usually want to understand:

  • Withdrawal and intoxication risk: how likely you are to have difficult withdrawal, and how severe it might be.
  • Physical health: any medical conditions that need attention.
  • Mental and emotional health: conditions such as depression, anxiety, trauma or others that affect recovery.
  • Readiness and motivation: how you feel about change right now.
  • Relapse risk: how likely you are to return to use without more support.
  • Recovery environment: whether your home, relationships, work and housing support recovery or make it harder.

The level of care recommended is meant to match the overall picture, and to change as that picture changes. A good assessment is not about putting you in the most expensive program, nor the cheapest. It is about the least intensive setting that is still safe and effective for you right now.

Inpatient vs outpatient rehab: who each suits

There is no universal rule, and only a qualified clinician who knows your situation can make a proper recommendation. But some general patterns are worth knowing.

Inpatient or residential care may suit you if:

  • You are at risk of serious withdrawal and need medical supervision.
  • You have tried outpatient treatment and found it was not enough to stop or cut down.
  • Your home is unsafe or unstable, or the people you live with are using.
  • You have significant mental health symptoms alongside substance use.
  • You need a full break from everyday pressures to focus on recovery.
  • You have serious physical health problems that need monitoring.

Outpatient care may suit you if:

  • You are medically stable and not at risk of dangerous withdrawal, or you have already completed supervised detox.
  • You have a reasonably safe, supportive place to live.
  • You are motivated and able to attend sessions regularly.
  • You need to keep working, studying or caring for others.
  • You are stepping down after a residential stay and want to continue with support.

When mental health is part of the picture

Substance use and mental health conditions often occur together. If that is true for you, look for a program at any level that treats both at the same time rather than one after the other. Our guide to dual diagnosis treatment explains what integrated care looks like and what to ask.

Cost and insurance, in general terms

Cost is a real part of this decision, and it is fine to say so. As a general rule, the more intensive the level of care, the more it tends to cost, because round-the-clock staffing, accommodation and medical care are expensive to provide. Outpatient care is usually the least expensive option, with intensive outpatient and day programs somewhere in between. Within each level, though, prices vary widely between programs, so comparing like with like matters.

Always ask for an itemised quote, so you can see exactly what is included and what is extra. Things like detox, medication, psychiatric appointments, family sessions and aftercare are sometimes billed separately.

Insurance

Insurance cover for addiction and mental health treatment depends on your plan and your country. In the US, many plans are required to cover mental health and substance use disorder services, and HealthCare.gov explains the basics of that coverage. Even so, plans often require pre-authorisation, may only cover certain levels of care or certain providers, and may limit the length of a stay. Before starting, it is worth calling your insurer and asking:

  • Which levels of care are covered, and is pre-authorisation required?
  • Is the program I am considering in network?
  • Are there limits on the number of days or sessions?
  • What will I pay out of pocket at each level?
  • What happens if my clinician recommends a step up or a step down?

In countries with public health systems, such as the UK, there may be free or low-cost options through your doctor or local services, although waiting times can vary. Our guide on how to pay for rehab covers funding options in more depth.

Work, family and everyday life

For many people, the practical side of life weighs as heavily as the clinical side. These considerations are real, and a good clinician will take them seriously.

Work and study

Outpatient and intensive outpatient programs are often designed around working hours, which can make it possible to keep your job while getting help. Residential care usually means time away. In some countries there are legal protections or leave entitlements that may apply when you take time off for medical treatment; in the US, for example, some employees may be eligible for job-protected leave. Rules vary, so check with your employer's HR team or an employment adviser, and remember that you generally do not have to share more detail than necessary.

It can be tempting to choose outpatient care simply because it fits around work. That is understandable, but if your health or safety genuinely needs more support, getting the right level of care now may protect your job and your health better in the long run.

Children and caring responsibilities

If you care for children or other relatives, residential treatment can feel impossible to arrange. Some people find that a day program or IOP is a workable compromise. Others arrange support from family or friends for a few weeks. A small number of programs can accommodate parents with children, so it is worth asking. Do not let guilt about caring responsibilities stop you from asking what is possible.

Family involvement

Family support can make a meaningful difference. Outpatient care makes it easier to involve family day to day, while many residential programs offer family sessions, visits or education days. If your relationships at home are part of what makes recovery hard, some distance during early treatment may help, followed by careful work on those relationships later on.

Your home environment

Ask yourself honestly: is home a place where recovery is possible right now? If alcohol or drugs are easy to get, if someone you live with is using, or if home is a source of conflict or danger, outpatient care may be much harder. Supportive or sober housing, combined with a day program or IOP, can be a useful middle ground.

Stepping down (and up) between levels

One of the most helpful ideas in modern treatment is that levels of care are not a one-way ticket. Many people start at a more intensive level and step down gradually as they become more stable. A common path might look like this:

  1. Medically supervised detox, if needed, to manage withdrawal safely.
  2. A period of residential treatment to build a foundation.
  3. A day program or partial hospitalisation for added structure while returning home.
  4. An intensive outpatient program for several weeks.
  5. Standard outpatient therapy and peer support for the longer term.

Not everyone needs every step. Some people start in outpatient care and do well. Others start in outpatient care and need to step up for a while. Neither is a failure. It simply means the plan is responding to what is actually happening, which is exactly what it should do.

The transition points, particularly leaving residential care, are often the most vulnerable times. Ask any program how they handle these handovers, and try to have your next appointment booked before you finish one level. The SAMHSA National Helpline can help people in the US find local services at different levels, and our article on life after rehab and aftercare covers what continuing support can look like.

A decision checklist

Use these questions to guide a conversation with your doctor, an assessor or a program. Writing down your answers beforehand can make the conversation easier.

About your health and safety

  • Am I at risk of difficult or dangerous withdrawal? Do I need medically supervised detox first?
  • Do I have physical health conditions that need monitoring?
  • Am I having thoughts of harming myself, or severe mental health symptoms?
  • Have I tried a less intensive level before, and what happened?

About your environment

  • Is my home safe and free of alcohol or drugs?
  • Do the people around me support my recovery?
  • Can I realistically attend several sessions a week?
  • What are my work, study and caring commitments, and how flexible are they?

Questions to ask a program

  • Which levels of care do you offer, and how do you decide which one is right for me?
  • Do you use a structured assessment such as the ASAM Criteria?
  • Who is on your clinical team, and what are their qualifications?
  • Is medical care available on site, and what happens in an emergency?
  • Do you treat mental health conditions alongside substance use?
  • How do you involve family?
  • Can I step up or down within your program, or will you refer me elsewhere?
  • What is included in the fee? Can I have an itemised quote?
  • What does your discharge and aftercare planning look like?

FAQ

Is inpatient rehab more effective than outpatient?

Not automatically. The most effective setting is the one that matches your needs. For someone who is medically stable with a supportive home, outpatient care can work well. For someone at risk of dangerous withdrawal, with an unsafe home or serious mental health symptoms, a more intensive level may be safer. A clinical assessment is the best way to decide, and the plan can change over time.

How long does inpatient rehab usually last?

It varies by program, by country and by person. Medically managed inpatient stays are often short and focused on stabilising, while residential programs commonly run for several weeks. Insurance can also affect length of stay. What matters most is that you stay long enough to benefit and that a clear plan is in place for continuing care afterwards, because recovery continues well beyond discharge.

Can I keep working during outpatient rehab?

Often, yes. Many intensive outpatient and standard outpatient programs offer evening or flexible sessions so people can continue working or studying. Day programs and partial hospitalisation usually take up most of the day, which can make working difficult. If you need time off, check your employer's policies and any legal leave protections where you live, and ask the program for documentation if you need it.

What is the difference between residential and inpatient rehab?

The terms are often used interchangeably, but there can be a difference. "Inpatient" sometimes refers specifically to hospital-based, medically managed care with round-the-clock doctors and nurses, while "residential" usually describes living at a treatment facility with structured therapy and varying levels of medical support. Ask any program exactly what medical care is available on site, day and night.

Do I need detox before outpatient treatment?

It depends on what you use, how much and for how long. If you are physically dependent on alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids, talk to a doctor before stopping, because withdrawal can be dangerous and may need medical supervision. Some withdrawal can be safely managed on an outpatient basis under medical care, while other situations need inpatient support. Never attempt alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal alone.

What if I choose the wrong level of care?

It is common to adjust along the way, and it does not mean you have failed. Good programs review your progress regularly and will recommend stepping up if you need more support, or stepping down when you are ready. Be honest with your care team about how things are going. The goal is to find the right fit for you at each stage, not to get it perfect from the start.

Getting help now

If you or someone you love is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. In the US, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call the free, confidential SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357, available 24/7. In the UK, call NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, find a local service at findahelpline.com.
