Watching someone you love struggle with alcohol or drugs is one of the loneliest experiences there is. You may feel frightened, angry, exhausted and guilty all in the same afternoon. And underneath it all is a question that does not have an easy answer: how to help someone go to rehab when they do not seem to want help, or are not ready to hear it.

The good news, and it is genuinely good news, is that families and friends have more influence than they often realise. Research over the past few decades has moved away from the idea that a person must hit "rock bottom" before anything can change, and towards approaches that teach loved ones how to communicate, how to reward healthier choices, and how to look after themselves along the way.

This guide walks you through the whole process calmly: recognising the signs, choosing the moment, what to say and what not to say, approaches with real evidence behind them, how to prepare treatment options in advance, where boundaries end and enabling begins, and what to do if things become an emergency.

Recognising the signs that someone may need help

Substance problems rarely announce themselves. They tend to arrive gradually, with small changes that are easy to explain away one at a time. It is the pattern, rather than any single incident, that usually tells the story. You might notice some of the following:

  • Changes in behaviour: secrecy, lying about where they have been, missing work or school, withdrawing from friends or hobbies they used to love.
  • Physical changes: poor sleep or sleeping at odd hours, weight changes, frequent illness, smelling of alcohol, unexplained injuries, or looking unwell in the mornings.
  • Emotional changes: irritability, mood swings, anxiety or low mood, defensiveness whenever drinking or drug use comes up.
  • Practical consequences: money going missing, unpaid bills, borrowing, trouble with the law, driving after drinking, or relationship conflict.
  • Loss of control: using more than they intended, failed attempts to cut down, or needing more to get the same effect.

None of these signs is proof on its own, and many overlap with depression, anxiety, grief or physical illness. Substance use and mental health difficulties also often travel together, which is why dual diagnosis treatment matters for so many people. You do not need to diagnose anyone. Your job is simply to notice, to care, and to open a door.

Before you talk: get clear on your goal

It helps to decide what you are actually hoping for from the first conversation. Most of the time, the realistic goal is not "they agree to go to rehab tonight". It is something smaller and more achievable: that they feel heard rather than attacked, that they know you are worried, and that they know help exists when they are ready.

Take a little time beforehand to ask yourself:

  • What specific things have I seen that worry me? (Concrete examples are more useful than general labels.)
  • What am I feeling? Fear, anger, sadness? Can I talk without that feeling taking over?
  • What would a good outcome look like this time?
  • Who else, if anyone, should be part of this, and who would make it feel like an ambush?
  • What will I do if the conversation goes badly?

Writing a few notes can help. So can talking it through first with a counsellor, a family support group, or a trusted friend who will not spread the news.

How to start the conversation

Choose your timing carefully

Timing matters more than perfect wording. Try to talk when your loved one is sober, when neither of you is rushed, and when you are both reasonably calm. Avoid raising it in the middle of an argument, at a party, when they are hungover and defensive, or in front of children or colleagues.

Sometimes a natural opening appears after a consequence: a missed family event, a frightening night, a conversation where they admit they are not feeling great. These moments can be gentle openings, as long as you approach them with care rather than "I told you so".

Use "I" statements

"You" statements tend to sound like accusations: "You are drinking too much", "You have ruined this family". "I" statements describe your own experience, which is much harder to argue with and much less likely to trigger shame:

  • "I have noticed you have been sleeping a lot less, and I am worried about you."
  • "I felt scared on Saturday when you drove home after the party."
  • "I miss spending evenings with you the way we used to."

Leave shame out of it

Shame is one of the strongest forces keeping people stuck. Many people who struggle with substances already feel deeply ashamed, and shaming language tends to deepen secrecy rather than change behaviour. Try to avoid labels such as "addict", "alcoholic" or "junkie", avoid comparisons to other people, and avoid dredging up every past mistake at once. Focus on a few specific, recent examples and on how much you care.

Listen more than you speak

Ask open questions and then really listen to the answers. "How have things been for you lately?" or "What do you like about drinking, and what do you not like about it?" can reveal far more than a prepared speech. You may hear things that surprise you: loneliness, pain, trauma, or a quiet wish to stop that they have not been able to say aloud.

Approaches with evidence: CRAFT and motivational interviewing

CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training)

CRAFT is a structured, skills-based programme designed specifically for the family members and friends of someone who is using substances and is not yet in treatment. It was developed by psychologist Robert Meyers and colleagues, building on the Community Reinforcement Approach. Rather than confronting the person, CRAFT teaches loved ones to change the environment around substance use so that sober, healthier behaviour becomes more rewarding and continued use becomes less comfortable.

CRAFT typically helps family members to:

  • Understand the patterns around use: the triggers, the times of day, the people and places involved, and what the person gets out of it.
  • Improve communication using positive, specific, non-blaming language.
  • Reinforce non-using behaviour by spending time together, offering warmth and attention when the person is sober.
  • Allow natural consequences of use to happen rather than rescuing the person from them, where it is safe to do so.
  • Recognise windows of opportunity when the person may be more open to treatment, and have a plan ready to act on them.
  • Stay safe, including planning for situations where there is a risk of violence.
  • Look after their own wellbeing, whether or not the person ever enters treatment.

Controlled studies have found CRAFT to be more effective at helping loved ones engage a resistant person in treatment than both confrontational interventions and referral to traditional family support groups alone, and family members taking part often report improvements in their own wellbeing too. CRAFT is usually delivered by a trained therapist, though self-guided workbooks based on the approach also exist. You can ask a local addiction service or therapist whether they offer it.

Motivational interviewing ideas you can borrow

Motivational interviewing is a counselling style used by clinicians to help people resolve mixed feelings about change. You do not need to be trained in it to borrow some of its spirit:

  • Expect ambivalence. Most people both want to change and do not want to. That is normal, not a sign of failure.
  • Roll with resistance. Arguing tends to make people defend their position. If they push back, acknowledge it rather than escalate.
  • Reflect what you hear. "It sounds like drinking helps you unwind, but you are not happy about how you feel the next day."
  • Draw out their own reasons. People are more persuaded by what they say than by what they are told. "What would be different if you cut back?"
  • Respect their autonomy. "It is your decision. I just want you to know I am here, and I have found some options if you ever want to look at them."

What about confrontational "interventions"?

Many people picture the television-style intervention: family members gathered in a room, letters read aloud, an ultimatum delivered, and the person driven straight to a facility. It is an understandable image, and it comes from a real place of love and desperation.

To be honest with you, though, the evidence for confrontational approaches is weak. Studies of surprise, confrontation-based interventions have found that many families who plan them do not go through with them, and that the approach can damage trust, increase shame and, in some cases, push people further away. Confrontation can also backfire in ways that are hard to predict when someone has an underlying mental health condition or a history of trauma.

That does not mean families should never come together, or that every structured family meeting is harmful. Some professionals run invitational, non-confrontational formats in which the person is told about the meeting in advance and invited to take part. If you are considering any kind of formal intervention, it is worth speaking with a qualified addiction professional first, and asking directly what the approach involves and what evidence supports it.

Prepare treatment options in advance

One of the most practical things you can do is to have options ready before the moment arrives. When someone finally says "Okay, maybe I need help", that window can close quickly. If you then spend a week ringing around, the moment may pass.

Some groundwork to do quietly in the background:

  • Understand the levels of care. Read up on inpatient vs outpatient rehab so you can talk about different options rather than presenting residential rehab as the only path.
  • Check whether medical detox is needed. Withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines can be dangerous and should never be attempted alone. Look for medically supervised programmes.
  • Shortlist a few facilities. Browse listings and our guide on how to choose a rehab. Note waiting times, admission requirements and what each programme treats.
  • Look into payment. Check insurance cover or public funding where you live, and ask any facility for an itemised quote. Our guide on how to pay for rehab covers the main routes.
  • Know the free helplines. In the US, the SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential and open 24/7, and can help with referrals.

A checklist of questions to ask a programme

  • Do you offer or arrange medically supervised detox, if it is needed?
  • Which licensed or registered clinicians are on staff, and what are their qualifications?
  • Do you treat co-occurring mental health conditions?
  • What evidence-based therapies do you use?
  • Do you offer medication for alcohol or opioid use disorder, or support people who already take it?
  • How are families involved in treatment?
  • What does aftercare planning look like?
  • How soon could someone be admitted, and what do they need to bring?
  • Can you give me an itemised quote in writing?

Boundaries vs enabling

These two words come up constantly in family support, and they can feel confusing. In simple terms:

  • Enabling is anything that, usually with loving intentions, shields the person from the consequences of their use or makes it easier to continue. Examples might include calling in sick for them, paying off debts caused by use, covering up for them with relatives, or buying alcohol "so at least they are drinking at home".
  • Boundaries are clear limits about what you will and will not do, which protect your own wellbeing. They are about your behaviour, not about controlling theirs.

A boundary might sound like: "I love you, and I am not going to give you money any more. I will happily buy groceries with you." Or: "If you come home drunk, I will go and stay at my sister's that night." The key is to state it calmly, mean it, and follow through without punishment or drama.

A few cautions. Boundaries are not threats, and they work best when they are realistic. Natural consequences should be allowed only when it is safe: never let someone drive impaired, and never leave someone alone if they may be at risk of overdose or harm. If you are worried about your own safety or that of children in the home, that comes first, and you can seek help from local domestic abuse services or emergency services.

Caring for yourself

You cannot pour from an empty cup, and supporting someone through addiction can take a real toll on your sleep, health, finances and relationships. Caring for yourself is not selfish. It makes you steadier, and a steadier presence is more helpful to the person you love.

Some places to find support:

  • Al-Anon and Alateen: long-established mutual support groups for families and friends of people who drink, with meetings in many countries and online.
  • Nar-Anon: a similar twelve-step fellowship for people affected by someone else's drug use.
  • SMART Recovery Family & Friends: a science-based programme that draws on CRAFT and cognitive-behavioural tools, offered in meetings and online.
  • Individual therapy: a counsellor can help you work through grief, anger and anxiety. If you are struggling with anxiety, low mood or burnout of your own, that deserves care too.

Day to day, protect small anchors: sleep, meals, time outside, a friend you can be honest with, and something that has nothing to do with your loved one's recovery. You are allowed to have a good day even when they are not.

What to do in an emergency

Sometimes the situation is not a conversation, it is a crisis. If you believe someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number straight away (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK, 112 across much of Europe).

Signs of a possible overdose

According to the CDC, signs of an opioid overdose can include:

  • Small, pinpoint pupils
  • Falling asleep or losing consciousness, and being impossible to wake
  • Slow, weak or stopped breathing
  • Choking or gurgling sounds
  • A limp body
  • Cold, clammy skin, or skin that looks pale, blue or grey, especially lips and fingertips

Alcohol poisoning can look similar: confusion, vomiting, seizures, slow or irregular breathing, low body temperature and being unable to wake up. Never leave someone to "sleep it off" if you are worried.

What to do

  1. Call emergency services immediately.
  2. If you suspect opioids and you have naloxone, give it according to the instructions on the package. Naloxone can reverse an opioid overdose and is available without a prescription in many places, including over the counter as a nasal spray in the US.
  3. Try to keep the person awake and breathing. If they are breathing but unresponsive, lay them on their side in the recovery position so they do not choke.
  4. Stay with them until help arrives. Naloxone can wear off before the opioids do, so medical care is still essential.

If your loved one uses opioids, or uses drugs that could be contaminated, keeping naloxone at home and learning how to use it is one of the most practical acts of love available to you. Ask a pharmacist or local health service about access where you live.

If someone talks about wanting to die or harming themselves, take it seriously. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. In the UK, you can call Samaritans on 116 123.

Legal options, in general terms

Families sometimes ask whether they can make an adult go to treatment. The honest answer is that it depends very much on where you live. Some jurisdictions have laws allowing involuntary assessment or treatment for substance use in limited circumstances, usually where there is a serious risk to the person or others, and usually through a court or medical process. Others have mental health laws that may apply only when there is a co-occurring psychiatric emergency. Some places have drug courts or diversion schemes that offer treatment as an alternative within the justice system.

These laws vary widely between countries, and between states or regions within countries. They also come with significant considerations around civil liberties, and outcomes are mixed. If you are considering this route, speak with a lawyer or a local health authority who knows the rules where you are, rather than relying on general information online.

If they say no (and what happens if they say yes)

If your loved one refuses help, try not to treat it as the end of the story. Many people need several conversations, over weeks or months, before they are ready. Keep the door open, keep your boundaries, keep looking after yourself, and keep your shortlist of options up to date. A "no" today is not a "no" forever.

If they say yes, act quickly but calmly. Make the call together if they want you to. Help with practical things like packing, arranging time off work or childcare, and transport. Then step back a little and let the clinicians take the lead. If it helps them feel less daunted, a quieter holistic setting may feel more approachable than they expect. Recovery is theirs to own, and your role shifts from persuading to supporting. Looking ahead, it is worth reading about life after rehab and aftercare, because the weeks after treatment matter as much as the treatment itself.

FAQ

How do I help someone go to rehab if they do not want to go?

Focus on connection rather than pressure. Talk when they are sober, use "I" statements, and listen to their reasons. Approaches like CRAFT teach families to reward sober behaviour, allow safe natural consequences, and recognise moments of openness. Have treatment options researched in advance so you can act quickly when they show interest, and get support for yourself in the meantime.

Do confrontational interventions work?

The evidence for surprise, confrontational interventions is weak. Many families who plan them do not go ahead, and the approach can damage trust and increase shame. Non-confrontational, skills-based methods such as CRAFT have stronger research support for helping loved ones engage someone in treatment. If you are considering a formal intervention, talk to a qualified addiction professional first.

What is the difference between helping and enabling?

Helping supports the person's wellbeing and recovery, such as offering a lift to an appointment or spending time together when they are sober. Enabling protects them from the consequences of use, such as paying debts caused by drinking or lying to their employer. Boundaries are about what you will and will not do, stated calmly and followed through without punishment.

Can I force an adult family member into rehab?

It depends on where you live. Some places allow involuntary assessment or treatment in limited circumstances, usually involving serious risk and a court or medical process, while others do not. The rules vary between countries and regions, so speak with a local lawyer or health authority. In an emergency, always call emergency services first.

Where can families get support for themselves?

Al-Anon and Alateen support people affected by someone else's drinking, and Nar-Anon supports those affected by drug use. SMART Recovery Family & Friends offers a science-based alternative drawing on CRAFT. A therapist can also help with the stress, grief and anger that often come with loving someone who is struggling.

Getting help now

If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call the SAMHSA National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). In the UK, call NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, find a local service at findahelpline.com.
