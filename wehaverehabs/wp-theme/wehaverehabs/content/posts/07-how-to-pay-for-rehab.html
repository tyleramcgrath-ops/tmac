Money is one of the most common reasons people put off getting help, and one of the least talked about. If you are wondering how to pay for rehab, you are not being shallow or practical at the wrong moment. You are asking a real question that deserves a clear answer, because the way treatment is funded shapes where you can go, how long you can stay and what happens afterwards.

The good news is that there are more routes than most people realise. Private insurance, public programs, charity and state-funded places, sliding-scale fees, workplace schemes and, in the UK, the NHS can all open doors. Self-pay is an option too, but it should be a considered choice rather than a default you fall into because nobody explained the alternatives.

This guide walks through each route in plain language. It does not quote prices, because costs vary enormously by country, setting, length of stay and level of medical care. What it will do is help you ask the right questions, read the answers carefully and avoid the traps that catch people when they are tired and frightened.

How to pay for rehab: start with the care you need

Before you work out funding, it helps to have a rough sense of the care you are funding. Payers, whether an insurer, a state program or the NHS, tend to fund treatment that matches a clinical assessment of need. A residential stay, an intensive outpatient program, a medically supervised detox and weekly counselling are very different things, and they are paid for differently.

A good first step is an assessment with your doctor, a local treatment service or an intake clinician at a program you are considering. Many services use the criteria developed by the American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) to match people to a level of care, and insurers in the US often refer to those same criteria when deciding what to approve.

If you are unsure which setting fits, our guides on inpatient vs outpatient rehab and what happens in medical detox are useful companions to this one. One safety point matters whatever your budget: withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines can be dangerous and should not be attempted alone. If cost is tempting you to skip medical supervision, talk to a doctor first; there are often lower-cost or publicly funded ways to detox safely.

Private health insurance in the US

For many people in the United States, private insurance is the first place to look, whether that is a plan through an employer, a plan bought on the Health Insurance Marketplace or a family member's policy.

Mental health and substance use are essential health benefits

Under the Affordable Care Act, most individual and small-group plans must cover a set of essential health benefits, and mental health and substance use disorder services are among them. That includes behavioural health treatment, counselling and psychotherapy, and inpatient services. HealthCare.gov explains this coverage in plain terms and is the best place to check what applies to Marketplace plans. Coverage still depends on your specific plan, so the details in your own policy documents matter more than any general rule.

Mental health parity

Federal mental health parity law generally means that when a plan covers mental health and substance use care, it should not impose limits that are more restrictive than those it places on comparable medical and surgical care. In practice, that covers things such as co-pays, visit limits and prior-authorisation rules. Parity does not force every plan to cover every kind of program, and some plan types are treated differently, but it gives you a basis to question limits that look unfair. If something seems out of step with how your plan treats physical health, it is reasonable to ask the insurer to explain, in writing, how the rule complies.

Questions to ask your insurer

Call the member services number on your card, or ask a program's admissions team to run a verification of benefits for you. Either way, write down the date, the name of the person you spoke to and a reference number. Useful questions include:

  • Is substance use or mental health treatment covered under my plan, and at which levels of care (detox, residential, partial hospitalisation, intensive outpatient, outpatient)?
  • Is this specific facility in network? If not, do I have any out-of-network benefits?
  • Does the treatment need prior authorisation, and who requests it?
  • What is my deductible, how much of it have I met, and what co-insurance or co-pays apply?
  • What is my out-of-pocket maximum for the year?
  • How are continued-stay reviews handled, and what happens if a review is denied?
  • Are prescriptions used in treatment covered under my pharmacy benefit?

Approval is often given in stages rather than for a whole stay at once. A program may be authorised for a set number of days and then reviewed. That is normal, but it is worth knowing in advance so a review does not feel like a crisis.

If a claim or authorisation is denied

Denials are not always final. Ask for the reason in writing, ask which criteria were used, and ask about the internal appeal process and any external review rights. The treating clinician can often help by providing records or speaking to the insurer's reviewer. Keep copies of everything.

Medicaid and Medicare

Public health coverage in the US can pay for a great deal of addiction and mental health treatment, though the details vary.

Medicaid

Medicaid is run jointly by the federal government and each state, so what it covers depends heavily on where you live. Many state Medicaid programs cover a range of substance use and mental health services, which can include assessment, counselling, outpatient and intensive outpatient care, medication for opioid use disorder and, in some states, residential treatment. Eligibility rules also differ by state. If your income has dropped, or you have never checked, it is worth applying; your state Medicaid agency or HealthCare.gov can point you in the right direction.

Not every private program accepts Medicaid, so ask early. Treatment providers that do accept it often have experience helping new patients through the paperwork.

Medicare

Medicare, the federal program mainly for people aged 65 and over and some younger people with disabilities, covers certain mental health and substance use services. Broadly, inpatient hospital care sits under Part A, outpatient services such as counselling and some program-based care sit under Part B, and prescriptions sit under Part D or a Medicare Advantage plan. Many holistic or luxury residential programs are not Medicare-approved providers, so check before you assume coverage. Medicare's own resources and your plan documents are the authoritative source.

State-funded programs and SAMHSA block grants

If you have no insurance, or your insurance does not cover what you need, publicly funded treatment is the route many people overlook.

Every US state has a single state agency responsible for substance use services. These agencies receive federal funding, including the Substance Use Prevention, Treatment, and Recovery Services Block Grant administered by SAMHSA, alongside state money. That funding is used to support treatment for people who cannot otherwise pay, with priority often given to certain groups such as pregnant women and people who inject drugs. Programs funded this way may be free or charge a reduced fee.

The trade-offs are real but manageable. There can be waiting lists, the settings are usually practical rather than luxurious, and you may not get to choose a specific program. But the clinical care can be solid, and starting somewhere is often better than waiting for the perfect option.

The simplest way in is to call the free, confidential SAMHSA National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357. It is open 24 hours a day, every day of the year, and can refer you to state-funded services and programs that use sliding-scale fees. You can also contact your state's substance use agency directly.

Sliding-scale fees, charity places and community programs

Many nonprofit and community treatment providers set their fees according to income and household size. This is called a sliding scale, and it can make outpatient counselling, intensive outpatient programs and sometimes residential care far more reachable than you might expect.

When you contact a provider, ask directly:

  • Do you offer a sliding scale, and what documents do you need to assess it?
  • Do you have any scholarship, charity or grant-funded places?
  • Are you a federally qualified health center or community behavioural health clinic, and does that affect fees?
  • Is there a waiting list, and is there anything I can access while I wait?

Faith-based organisations, mutual-aid groups and recovery community centres can also be part of the picture. Peer support groups are typically free and can carry you through a waiting period, though they are not a substitute for medical care when withdrawal or serious mental health symptoms are involved.

Employer schemes and Employee Assistance Programs

If you work, check whether your employer offers an Employee Assistance Program, often shortened to EAP. These are confidential services, usually provided by an outside company, that offer a limited number of free counselling sessions and help with referrals. An EAP will rarely fund a full residential stay, but it can give you an assessment, help you understand your insurance and connect you with appropriate care quickly.

Many people worry that using an EAP will get back to their manager. Reputable EAPs operate under confidentiality rules and do not report individual use to employers, except in narrow circumstances such as an immediate safety risk. If you are concerned, ask the EAP to explain its confidentiality policy before you share details.

It is also worth knowing about workplace protections. In the US, the Family and Medical Leave Act may allow eligible employees to take job-protected, unpaid leave for treatment, and disability laws may offer some protections. In the UK and elsewhere, sick leave and occupational health routes may apply. An HR department or an employment adviser can explain what applies to you.

The NHS route in the UK

If you live in the UK, NHS-funded drug and alcohol treatment is free at the point of use. It does not usually look like a private residential retreat, but it can be effective and it is designed to keep people safe.

How to access it

  • Talk to your GP. Your GP can assess you, discuss safe options for reducing or stopping, prescribe where appropriate and refer you to local services.
  • Self-refer to a local drug and alcohol service. In much of England, you can contact community drug and alcohol services directly, without going through a GP. The NHS guidance on getting help for drug addiction explains how to find one.
  • Ask about detox and residential options. Community services usually provide counselling, group work and medically supported withdrawal. Funding for inpatient detox or residential rehab is typically arranged through your local service and depends on your assessed needs and local commissioning.

Waiting times and the range of services vary between areas, and the process for residential funding can take time. Ask your keyworker what the steps are, what you can do in the meantime and whether there are local charities offering additional support. Mental health care is accessed in a similar way, through your GP or, in England, through NHS Talking Therapies, which accept self-referrals.

Some people combine routes: they start with NHS community support and then pay privately for a residential stay, or they go private for a short period and return to NHS services for long-term aftercare. If you do this, let both sides know, so your care stays joined up.

Paying for rehab yourself

Self-pay gives you the widest choice. You can choose a program for its approach, its setting or its specialism, without waiting for approval. It can also be the only route when a program is abroad or outside insurance networks. But it is a serious financial commitment, and it should be entered into with the same care you would give any large purchase.

Get an itemised quote in writing

Ask every program you are considering for a written, itemised quote. A headline figure tells you very little. A good quote will separate out:

  • Accommodation and meals, per night or per week
  • Medical detox, including doctor and nursing cover
  • Psychiatric assessment and medication reviews
  • Individual therapy sessions and how many are included each week
  • Group programs, family sessions and any specialist therapies
  • Lab tests, prescriptions and any off-site medical care
  • Transfers, airport pick-ups and any escort services
  • Aftercare, follow-up sessions or alumni support

Then ask what is not included. Extras such as outside medical appointments, certain medications, holistic add-ons and extended stays are where unexpected costs tend to appear.

A checklist of questions before you pay

  • What exactly does the quoted fee cover, and what costs extra?
  • Is payment required in full upfront, or can it be staged?
  • What is the refund policy if I leave early, need hospital care or the program is not a good fit?
  • What happens to my money if I need a higher level of medical care than you can provide?
  • Will you give me a receipt or invoice suitable for an insurance claim or tax purposes?
  • Who is the clinical lead, and what are their qualifications?
  • Is the program licensed or registered, and with which body?
  • Can I see the written terms and contract before I commit?

Even if a program is out of network, it may be possible to claim some costs back from your insurer. Ask the provider whether it supplies the coded invoices insurers usually need, and check with your insurer before treatment rather than after.

Other ways people fund self-pay

Some people use savings, help from family or the sale of an asset. Health savings accounts or flexible spending accounts in the US may cover some qualifying medical expenses, so check your plan's rules. If family members are contributing, it can help to agree in writing who is paying for what, so money does not become a source of conflict during an already tender time. Avoiding predatory financing and patient brokering

Unfortunately, the urgency people feel when seeking treatment can be exploited. A few warning signs are worth knowing.

High-pressure finance offers

Be cautious about loans or credit arrangements offered on the spot by a program or a third party, especially if you are asked to sign the same day. Read the interest rate, the total amount repayable and what happens if you leave treatment early. A reputable program will give you time to think and will not make admission conditional on taking out a particular loan.

Brokers and "free" travel

Be wary of anyone who offers free flights, free rent or cash to attend a specific program, or who seems more interested in your insurance details than your health. Some call centres and websites route people to whichever program pays them a fee. In many US states, patient brokering is illegal. Genuine helplines, such as the SAMHSA National Helpline, do not take payment to steer you to a particular provider.

Other red flags

  • Guarantees of a cure or a fixed success rate
  • Refusal to provide a written quote, contract or refund policy
  • Vague answers about licensing or the clinical team
  • Pressure to commit before you have had an assessment
  • Requests for payment in cash, cryptocurrency or to personal accounts

If something feels wrong, pause. A day spent checking credentials will rarely change the outcome of your recovery, but a bad contract can follow you for years.

What is usually not covered abroad

Treatment outside your home country appeals to many people, and it can be the right choice. Our guide to rehab abroad looks at it in depth. From a funding point of view, though, it comes with specific limits.

  • Most domestic insurance will not pay. US private plans, Medicaid and Medicare rarely cover residential treatment overseas, and NHS funding does not usually extend to private programs abroad.
  • Travel insurance typically excludes it. Standard travel policies often exclude pre-existing conditions and treatment you travel specifically to receive. Some also exclude incidents related to alcohol or drugs. Read the wording carefully and ask the insurer in writing.
  • Emergency care may be billed separately. If you need a hospital stay during treatment, the local hospital may charge you directly, even if the program fee was paid in full.
  • Medication and aftercare at home. Your home coverage may help with outpatient aftercare once you return, but check before you travel so the plan is in place.
  • Currency and refunds. Fees may be set in another currency, and refund terms may be governed by another country's law. Get everything in writing.

If you are considering a program overseas, budget for the whole journey, not just the stay: flights, any escort, a possible hospital visit, and aftercare when you come home.

FAQ

Does health insurance cover rehab?

Often, yes. In the US, most Affordable Care Act plans must cover mental health and substance use disorder services as essential health benefits, and parity law limits unfair restrictions. But coverage depends on your plan, the level of care and whether the provider is in network. Call your insurer or ask a program to verify your benefits, and get the key details, including prior-authorisation rules, in writing.

How can I get rehab if I have no insurance and little money?

Start with the SAMHSA National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357, which can refer you to state-funded programs and providers with sliding-scale fees. You can also apply for Medicaid, contact your state substance use agency or approach community health centres. In the UK, NHS drug and alcohol services are free and you can usually self-refer. Waiting lists can exist, so ask what support is available meanwhile.

Is NHS rehab free?

NHS-funded drug and alcohol treatment is free at the point of use. Most of it is community-based, including counselling, group work and medically supported withdrawal. Residential rehab can be funded in some cases, depending on your assessed needs and local arrangements, and the process can take time. Speak to your GP or contact your local drug and alcohol service to begin.

Will my employer find out if I use an EAP?

Employee Assistance Programs are designed to be confidential, and reputable providers do not tell employers which individuals use them, apart from narrow exceptions such as an immediate risk to safety. Your employer may receive anonymous usage statistics. If you are unsure, ask the EAP to explain its confidentiality policy before sharing personal details, and check your company's own policy too.

Will insurance pay for rehab abroad?

Usually not. Most US private plans, Medicaid, Medicare and NHS funding do not cover private residential treatment overseas, and standard travel insurance often excludes it. A few international health policies may offer some cover, so read the wording carefully and ask in writing. Budget for possible emergency hospital costs and for aftercare once you return home.

What should be in a rehab quote?

A good quote is itemised and in writing. It should separate accommodation, medical detox, psychiatric care, therapy sessions, medications, tests, transfers and aftercare, and it should clearly state what is not included. Ask about payment schedules, refunds if you leave early or need hospital care, and whether you will receive invoices suitable for insurance claims.

Getting help now

If you are in the US and in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. For free, confidential treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357, available 24/7. In the UK, call NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, find a local line at findahelpline.com. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.
