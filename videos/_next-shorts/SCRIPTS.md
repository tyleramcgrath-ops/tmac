## 1. Why Rich People Lease Supercars

**YouTube title:** Why Millionaires Lease Their $300K Supercars
**Format:** hook, then 5 beats, then climax, then comment bait

### Narration (~104 words)

> A three-hundred-thousand-dollar supercar can lose eighty-five grand just sitting in your garage.
>
> Exotic cars shed about twenty-eight percent of their value in five years.
>
> Now the tax myth. A supercar under six thousand pounds? First-year write-off caps near twenty thousand.
>
> A heavy SUV over six thousand pounds? Business use can write off up to one hundred percent in year one.
>
> Leasing anything over sixty-two thousand? The IRS claws some back.
>
> The real play is the cash. Three hundred K invested at seven percent is about one hundred twenty thousand in five years.
>
> Lease or buy? Tell me below. And ask a tax pro first.

### On-screen text (per beat)

| Beat | Big number | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | **-$85,000** | on a $300K supercar, 5 yrs |
| 1 | **28%** | avg exotic-car value lost in 5 yrs |
| 2 | **$20,200** | yr-1 depreciation cap: cars under 6,000 lb |
| 3 | **100%** | yr-1 write-off possible: SUVs over 6,000 lb GVWR (business use) |
| 4 | **$62,000+** | lease value that triggers IRS "lease inclusion" |
| Climax | **+$120,000** | $300K left invested, 7%/yr, 5 yrs (est.) |
| End | **LEASE or BUY?** | Not tax advice. Talk to a CPA. |

### Math and caveats
- Depreciation: $300,000 × 28.2% ≈ $84,600, rounded to "$85K". 28.2% is iSeeCars' current 5-year figure for all exotic cars. iSeeCars refreshes the number; a different snapshot showed 31.6%.
- Passenger-car (≤6,000 lb GVWR) first-year cap with bonus depreciation, 2025: $20,200. Without bonus: $12,200. Years 2 and 3: $19,600 and $11,800 (Rev. Proc. 2025-16).
- Heavy SUVs (6,000–14,000 lb GVWR): §179 is capped at $31,300 for 2025. The remainder can qualify for 100% bonus depreciation if placed in service after Jan 19, 2025 (OBBBA). This requires more than 50% business use, and the deduction is prorated by business-use percentage.
- Lease inclusion applies to passenger cars leased in 2025 with FMV above $62,000 (Rev. Proc. 2025-16).
- Opportunity cost **(est.)**: $300,000 × (1.07^5 − 1) ≈ $120,800. This assumes a 7% annual return and is not guaranteed. A lease still has a money factor (interest) and you still pay depreciation through the payment. The lease's advantages are liquidity and shifting the risk of a bigger-than-expected value drop, not a free car.

### Description
Leasing a supercar isn't a flex. It's a cash-flow decision. The real depreciation numbers, the tax rules influencers get wrong (the 6,000 lb line), and what $300K could earn if it stayed invested. Not tax advice.

## 2. What a Private Jet Really Costs Per Hour

**YouTube title:** What a Private Jet Really Costs Per Hour
**Format:** hook, then 4 beats, then climax, then comment bait

### Narration (~107 words)

> Owning a private jet can look cheaper per hour than renting one. Here's the catch.
>
> A new Embraer Phenom three hundred E lists around nine and a half million dollars.
>
> Before it flies a single hour: about three hundred sixty thousand a year in crew, hangar, insurance and management.
>
> Fly two hundred hours: roughly thirty-four hundred an hour. Fly a hundred? Over fifty-two hundred.
>
> A one-sixteenth NetJets share: up to eight hundred fifty K upfront, around thirteen K a month, plus eighty-five hundred per hour. That's nearly twelve thousand an hour.
>
> A jet card? About eighty-six hundred an hour. No hangar. No crew.
>
> Still want the jet?

### On-screen text (per beat)

| Beat | Big number | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | **OWN vs RENT** | per flight hour |
| 1 | **$9.45M** | new Phenom 300E list price |
| 2 | **$359K / yr** | fixed costs: crew, hangar, insurance, mgmt |
| 3 | **$3,448 / hr** | owner operating cost at 200 hrs/yr (ex-purchase) |
| 3b | **$5,243 / hr** | same jet at 100 hrs/yr |
| 4 | **~$11,700 / hr** | 1/16 fractional, fees + hourly, ex-share price (est.) |
| Climax | **$8,563 / hr** | avg light-jet card rate, Q1 2026 |
| End | **Still want the jet?** | |

### Math and caveats
- Phenom 300E figures from Flycraft: list $9.45M; fixed $359,130/yr (crew $201,380, training $26,250, hangar $31,474, insurance $22,026, management $48,000, misc $30,000); variable $1,652/hr. Totals: $524,330 at 100 hrs = $5,243/hr; $689,530 at 200 hrs = $3,448/hr.
- **The owner figures exclude the $9.45M purchase, depreciation and cost of capital.** If you also count 5% annual opportunity cost on $9.45M (our estimate, ≈ $472K/yr), the all-in figure becomes ≈ $5,800/hr at 200 hrs and ≈ $10,000/hr at 100 hrs. This could be added as a follow-up Short ("the hidden $472K").
- Fractional **(est.)**: Flycraft gives a 1/16 light-jet share (Phenom 300) at ~$500K–$850K for ~50 hrs/yr, $12K–$15K/month management, ~$8,500/occupied hour. Using the midpoint: ($13,500 × 12 + 50 × $8,500) / 50 = $11,740/hr, before the share's own cost or depreciation. Real contracts vary.
- Jet card: Private Jet Card Comparisons Q1 2026 averages are light $8,563/hr and midsize $9,639/hr. These include fuel surcharges and 7.5% FET.

Own, fractional, or jet card? The real per-hour math on a light jet: purchase price, the $359K/year you pay before takeoff, NetJets-style shares, and 2026 jet card rates.

## 3. Watches vs Supercars: Which Actually Holds Value

**YouTube title:** Rolex vs Lamborghini: Which Actually Holds Value?
**Format:** hook, then 4 beats, then climax, then comment bait

>
> A steel Rolex Daytona retails for sixteen thousand nine hundred. On the resale market? About thirty-two thousand.
>
> A Patek Nautilus fifty-seven eleven retailed near thirty-five thousand. Today it trades around one hundred twenty-three thousand.
>
> And watches fall too. The Rolex index is still down about eight percent over five years.

### Math and caveats
- Urus: Jalopnik (Jan 2026, using iSeeCars) gives a 2020 Urus MSRP of $211,321 incl. destination, a calculated residual of $139,260, and 34.1% depreciation. KBB dealer asks start near $175,000, so real-world loss may be smaller. **Fairness note:** not every exotic is this bad. iSeeCars shows the Ferrari 296 GTB at only ~17.7% over five years, and all exotics average ~28%.
- Daytona: WatchCharts gives retail $16,900 and market $32,003 (Sept 29, 2026), so +89%. Market price varies by dial ($28.8K black / $34.5K white). WatchScanning (July 2026) gives a market range of $24K–$30K+, so "about thirty-two" sits at the higher end.
- Nautilus 5711/1A: discontinued in 2021. Last retail $34,890 (Dec 2021); WatchCharts market ~$123,675 (Sept 30, 2026). This is down from 2022 peak prices reported at $150K–$200K+ (secondary source).
- Rolex index: WatchCharts 30-model Rolex index was 28,707 on June 27, 2026, +6.7% over 1 yr and −7.9% over 5 yrs.
- I deliberately left out the Submariner. Retail figures conflict across sources ($10,050–$11,350) and its premium is small or near zero.

## 4. Looks Rich vs Is Rich

**YouTube title:** Same $150K Salary. One Ends Up $236K Richer.
**Format:** hook, then 4 beats, then climax, then comment bait

### Narration (~108 words)

> Two people earn one hundred fifty thousand a year. Ten years later, one is two hundred thirty-six thousand dollars richer.
>
> Person A finances a one-hundred-twenty-thousand-dollar car. Six years, seven percent, the average new-car rate. That's about two thousand forty-six a month.
>
> Person B finances a forty-thousand-dollar car. Same terms. Six hundred eighty-two a month.
>
> B invests the difference: thirteen hundred sixty-four dollars every month, for ten years, as each car gets replaced.
>
> That's one hundred sixty-four thousand put in. At a seven percent real return, it grows to about two hundred thirty-six thousand in today's dollars.
>
> Same salary. One looks rich. One is rich.
>
> Which one are you?

### On-screen text (per beat)

| Beat | Big number | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | **$236,000** | the 10-year gap |
| 1 | **$2,046 / mo** | $120K car, 72 mo @ 7% APR |
| 2 | **$682 / mo** | $40K car, same terms |
| 3 | **$1,364 / mo** | invested instead |
| 4 | **$163,670** | total contributed, 10 yrs |
| Climax | **$236,074** | at 7% real (≈ $279K at 10% nominal) |
| End | **LOOKS RICH or IS RICH?** | |

### Math (show in pinned comment)
- Loan payment formula: P × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r = 7%/12 and n = 72.
  - $120,000 gives **$2,045.88/mo**. $40,000 gives **$681.96/mo**. Difference **$1,363.92/mo**.
- Assumption: each person replaces the car every 6 years at the same price and financing, so the payment gap runs all 120 months.
- Future value of $1,363.92/mo for 120 months, compounded monthly:
  - at **7% real**: **$236,074** (in today's dollars)
  - at **10% nominal** (S&P long-run average): **$279,392**
  - Total contributed: $163,670.
- 7% APR = Edmunds Q3 2026 average new-vehicle APR. The $40K car is close to Edmunds' average amount financed ($44,664).
- **Not counted (est.):** car equity at year 10. Each person is 4 years into a second loan, with balances of $45,695 (A) and $15,232 (B). If both cars keep ~60% of value after 4 years (our assumption, loosely based on iSeeCars' ~26–42% average 5-yr loss), A's extra equity is ≈ $17.5K, which leaves a net gap of ≈ $218K. Still "over two hundred thousand." Insurance, tax and registration on the pricier car are also ignored; including them would widen the gap.
- Returns are historical averages, not guaranteed. Real returns since 1957 are ~6.5–7%.

### Description
Same $150K income, two different cars, ten years. The exact math on what a $120K car payment really costs you, and where the difference goes if you invest it. Assumptions in the pinned comment.
